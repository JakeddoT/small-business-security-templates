Password and MFA policy template for small business

By William Todd, IT support and cybersecurity engineer · Updated 2026-11-21

A modern small business password policy comes down to five rules: long passphrases (15+ characters), a unique password for every account stored in a company password manager, MFA on everything that matters, no forced periodic changes, and separate admin accounts. The outline below follows current NIST guidance (SP 800-63B) and what cyber insurers ask about, written so you can adapt it into your own policy.

Password rules changed a lot in the last few years. Forced 90-day changes and "one uppercase, one symbol" rules turned out to make passwords weaker, because people respond with Summer2026!, then Fall2026!. Current guidance favors length, uniqueness and MFA instead.

Policy outline (adapt to your business)

1. Purpose and scope

Applies to all staff, contractors and service accounts that access company systems, including email, cloud apps, Wi-Fi, computers and phones used for work.

2. Password requirements

  • Length: at least 15 characters for any password used on its own. Passphrases of four or more random words are encouraged. Where a system enforces MFA, a shorter minimum (NIST allows 8) is acceptable, but 12+ is a sensible floor.
  • No complexity rules or forced rotation. Change a password only when there's reason to think it's compromised, or when someone with access leaves.
  • Unique for every system. Never reuse a work password anywhere, including personal accounts.
  • Blocked passwords: systems should reject common and previously breached passwords where they support it (Microsoft 365 and Google Workspace do some of this by default).

3. Password manager

  • The company provides a business password manager. Staff store all work passwords there, not in browsers, spreadsheets, sticky notes or email.
  • Shared logins (the front-desk account, the vendor portal) live in shared vaults with named access, so they can be removed in one step when someone leaves.
  • The password manager itself is protected with a strong master passphrase and MFA.

4. Multi-factor authentication (MFA)

  • Required on: email for every user, VPN and all remote access, admin accounts, banking and payroll, accounting, cloud file storage, and any system holding customer data.
  • Preferred methods: an authenticator app with number matching, or a security key / passkey. SMS codes only where nothing else is supported, and never for admin accounts.
  • Never approve an MFA prompt you didn't start. Deny it and report it the same day; it means someone has your password.

5. Admin and service accounts

  • Admins use a separate admin account for admin tasks and a normal account for email and browsing.
  • Admin accounts are listed, have MFA, and are reviewed every 90 days. Unused ones are disabled.
  • Service accounts (scanners, backup software, integrations) have long random passwords stored in the password manager and an owner who is responsible for them.

6. Exceptions

Any system that can't meet this policy is listed in an exceptions log with the reason, a compensating control, an owner and a review date. Exceptions are reviewed at least yearly and before every cyber insurance renewal.

7. Leavers and compromise

  • When someone leaves, their accounts are disabled the same day, and every shared password they knew is changed. See the offboarding checklist.
  • Suspected compromise: change the password, sign out all sessions, check MFA methods, and report it. For mailboxes, follow the hacked Microsoft 365 checklist.

Rolling it out without a revolt

  1. Pick and pay for a business password manager first. The policy is unworkable without one.
  2. Turn on MFA in stages, starting with admins and email. The MFA rollout plan includes a staff announcement email.
  3. Give staff 30 minutes to move their passwords into the manager, with help on hand.
  4. Have everyone sign the policy, and record it with your training log.

Frequently asked questions

Should employees be forced to change passwords every 90 days?

Current guidance from NIST recommends against routine forced rotation; change passwords when compromise is suspected instead.

Is SMS acceptable for MFA?

SMS is better than nothing, but authenticator apps or hardware keys are more resistant to phishing and SIM swapping.

How long should a business password be?

Current NIST guidance sets 15 characters as the minimum for a password used on its own. With MFA on the account, a shorter minimum is allowed, but long passphrases are still the easiest strong option.

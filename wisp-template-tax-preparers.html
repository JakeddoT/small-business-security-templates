WISP for tax preparers: what to include

By William Todd, IT support and cybersecurity engineer · Updated 2026-11-21

If you prepare tax returns for pay, you are expected to have a written information security plan (WISP). The FTC treats tax preparers as financial institutions under the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act, which brings them under the FTC Safeguards Rule (16 CFR Part 314). The IRS has also written its own guide for tax professionals, Publication 5708, Creating a Written Information Security Plan for your Tax & Accounting Practice. This page explains in plain English what a WISP has to contain and how a small practice can write one.

What a WISP must include under the Safeguards Rule

  1. A qualified individual. Name one person who is responsible for the program. In a small practice this is usually the owner.
  2. A written risk assessment. List where client data lives (tax software, email, scanned files, paper, backups) and what could go wrong with each.
  3. Safeguards for the risks you find. These include access controls, encryption of client data at rest and in transit, multi-factor authentication for anyone accessing client information, secure disposal, and change management.
  4. Monitoring and testing. Show how you check that your safeguards actually work, such as reviewing logs and running vulnerability scans.
  5. Staff training. Security awareness training for everyone who handles client data, with records that prove it happened.
  6. Service provider oversight. Choose vendors that can protect the data, require it in contracts, and review them periodically.
  7. A written incident response plan. Who does what when data is stolen or a system is compromised.
  8. Regular updates and reporting. Review the plan when your practice changes, and have the qualified individual report on it at least once a year.

Firms that maintain information on fewer than 5,000 consumers are exempt from some of the documentation requirements, including the written risk assessment, the written incident response plan, and the annual report. You still need the safeguards themselves. Writing these documents anyway is cheap insurance: it is what you will be asked for after an incident.

Breach notification to the FTC

Since May 2024, covered firms must notify the FTC within 30 days of discovering a security event that involves unencrypted information of 500 or more consumers. Also tell the IRS through your Stakeholder Liaison, and check your state's notification laws.

A simple WISP outline for a 1 to 10 person tax office

  1. Purpose and scope: which data, systems, and people the plan covers
  2. Qualified individual and backup contact
  3. Data inventory: tax software, cloud storage, email, paper files, retention periods
  4. Risk assessment table: risk, likelihood, impact, safeguard
  5. Access control and MFA requirements, including for remote access and tax software
  6. Device security: encryption, updates, antivirus, screen locks
  7. Email and phishing rules, including verifying refund or bank-detail changes
  8. Backup and restore procedure
  9. Vendor list and review schedule
  10. Training schedule and log
  11. Incident response steps and contacts (IRS, FTC, state, insurer, IT)
  12. Secure disposal of paper and devices
  13. Annual review sign-off

Frequently asked questions

Do tax preparers legally need a WISP?

Paid tax preparers are covered by the FTC Safeguards Rule, which requires a written information security program. IRS Publication 5708 provides guidance on creating one.

What is IRS Publication 5708?

An IRS guide that helps tax and accounting practices create a written information security plan. It includes a sample template.

How long does a WISP need to be?

There is no required length. A small practice can usually cover the required elements in 8 to 15 pages if the plan reflects how the office really works.

Is a WISP the same as a privacy policy?

No. A privacy policy tells clients how you use their data. A WISP is an internal plan describing how you protect it.

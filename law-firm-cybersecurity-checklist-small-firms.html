Law firm cybersecurity checklist for small and solo firms

By William Todd, IT support and cybersecurity engineer · Updated 2026-11-21

For a small or solo law firm, the cybersecurity basics are: MFA on email and practice management, full-disk encryption on every laptop and phone, a password manager, a secure way to share documents with clients, a call-back rule for any change to payment or trust account wiring instructions, tested backups, prompt offboarding, and a written incident plan. These map directly onto lawyers' ethical duties to protect client information and to understand the technology they use.

Under the ABA Model Rules, Rule 1.6(c) asks lawyers to make reasonable efforts to prevent unauthorized access to or disclosure of client information, and Comment 8 to Rule 1.1 includes understanding the benefits and risks of relevant technology as part of competence. ABA Formal Opinion 477R discusses securing client communications, and Formal Opinion 483 addresses lawyers' obligations after a data breach. Your state's version of the rules and its ethics opinions are what apply to you, so check them; this page is general information, not legal or ethics advice.

The threats small firms actually see

  • Email account takeover leading to fake payment instructions sent to clients or opposing counsel, especially around settlements and real estate closings (see real estate wire fraud).
  • Fake "new client" scams, where a supposed client sends a counterfeit check for a settlement or retainer and asks for part of it to be wired back before the check bounces. Verify funds have actually cleared with your bank, not just that they show as available.
  • Ransomware through phishing or exposed remote access (see checking Remote Desktop exposure).
  • Lost or stolen laptops holding client files, where encryption decides whether it's a minor event or a breach (see lost laptop steps).
  • Misdirected email with privileged documents (see sent email to the wrong person).

Tell clients how you'll communicate

Put a short paragraph in your engagement letter: how you will share documents, that you'll never change payment instructions by email, and how clients should confirm any request for money. It protects them and gives you a documented, reasonable practice.

Insurance and client questionnaires

Malpractice carriers, cyber insurers and larger corporate clients increasingly ask about MFA, encryption, backups and training. Keeping this checklist current makes those questionnaires faster (see the cyber insurance questionnaire guide).

Frequently asked questions

What cybersecurity is a small law firm expected to have?

Reasonable measures to protect client information. For most small firms that means MFA on email and practice software, encrypted devices, a password manager, secure document sharing, tested backups, training and an incident plan. Check your state's rules and ethics opinions.

Which ABA rules cover law firm cybersecurity?

Model Rule 1.6(c) on reasonable efforts to protect client information and Comment 8 to Rule 1.1 on technology competence, with Formal Opinions 477R and 483 on securing communications and responding to breaches. States adopt their own versions.

How do law firms prevent settlement wire fraud?

Never change payment instructions by email, confirm any change by phone on a number you already have, warn clients in the engagement letter, and use MFA on all email accounts.

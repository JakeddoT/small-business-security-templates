FTC Safeguards Rule checklist for small businesses

By Jake Todd, IT support and cybersecurity engineer · Updated 2026-11-21

The FTC Safeguards Rule (16 CFR Part 314) requires many non-bank financial businesses to run a written information security program. Lots of small firms don't realize they're covered until a client, lender or insurer asks. This page explains who's in scope and turns the requirements into a checklist.

Who the Safeguards Rule covers

It applies to businesses that are "financial institutions" under the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act and fall under FTC jurisdiction rather than a banking regulator. Common small-business examples include:

  • Tax preparers and many accounting firms
  • Mortgage brokers and non-bank lenders
  • Motor vehicle dealers that arrange financing or leasing
  • Payday lenders, check cashers, and wire transfer services
  • Collection agencies and credit counselors
  • Some investment advisers that are not registered with the SEC

If you collect customer financial information to provide a financial product or service, assume you might be covered and check with your attorney or industry association.

The checklist

  1. Designate a qualified individual to oversee the program (an employee or a service provider).
  2. Do a written risk assessment of where customer information is stored and how it could be exposed.
  3. Put safeguards in place:
    • Access controls, so people only see what their job needs
    • A data and systems inventory
    • Encryption of customer information at rest and in transit
    • Secure development practices for any in-house apps
    • Multi-factor authentication for anyone accessing information systems
    • Secure disposal, generally within two years of last use unless you have a business need
    • Change management, and monitoring of user activity
  4. Test and monitor: continuous monitoring, or an annual penetration test plus vulnerability scans every six months.
  5. Train staff on security awareness, and keep records.
  6. Oversee service providers: choose capable vendors, require safeguards in contracts, and review them periodically.
  7. Keep the program current as your business and risks change.
  8. Write an incident response plan.
  9. Report annually in writing to your board or owner.

The under-5,000-customer exemption

If you maintain customer information on fewer than 5,000 consumers, you're exempt from a few documentation-heavy items: the written risk assessment, continuous monitoring or annual pen testing, the written incident response plan, and the annual written report. The other safeguards still apply, including MFA, encryption, training and vendor oversight.

Breach notification

Since May 2024, covered businesses must notify the FTC within 30 days of discovering a notification event involving unencrypted information of at least 500 consumers. State breach laws may also apply.

Frequently asked questions

Who has to comply with the FTC Safeguards Rule?

Non-bank financial institutions under FTC jurisdiction, such as tax preparers, mortgage brokers, auto dealers that arrange financing, payday lenders, and collection agencies.

Is there a small business exemption from the Safeguards Rule?

Businesses with customer information on fewer than 5,000 consumers are exempt from some written documentation and testing requirements, but not from the core safeguards.

Does the Safeguards Rule require MFA?

Yes. It requires multi-factor authentication for any individual accessing any information system, unless the qualified individual approves an equivalent control in writing.

What happens if you don't comply with the Safeguards Rule?

The FTC can bring enforcement actions. Non-compliance can also hurt you with lenders, partners, and cyber insurers after an incident.

Data retention policy template for small business: classification and schedule

By William Todd, IT support and cybersecurity engineer · Updated 2026-11-21

A data retention policy says what kinds of data your business keeps, how long it keeps each kind, and how it's destroyed afterwards. For a small business, it's a short classification scheme (four levels is plenty), a retention schedule table, and a disposal rule. Keeping everything forever feels safe, but it means a breach exposes ten years of customer records instead of two.

Retention periods depend on your industry, state and contracts, so the schedule below is an example to confirm with your accountant and lawyer, not legal advice.

Step 1: Classify your data (4 levels)

LevelExamplesHandling
PublicWebsite text, brochures, price listsNo restrictions
InternalSchedules, internal procedures, policiesStaff only; not posted publicly
ConfidentialContracts, customer lists, financials, payrollNeed-to-know access; approved storage only; not emailed outside unencrypted
RestrictedCard data, Social Security numbers, tax returns, health information, bank detailsEncrypted at rest and in transit; access logged; minimum number of people; never on personal devices

When in doubt, classify up. A spreadsheet with customer names and SSNs is Restricted, even if most columns are harmless.

Step 2: Set the retention schedule

Record typeCommon retention (confirm for your situation)Source of the requirement
Tax returns and supporting recordsGenerally 3 years after filing; longer (up to 7 years) in some situationsIRS record-keeping guidance
Employment tax recordsAt least 4 yearsIRS
Payroll recordsAt least 3 yearsU.S. Department of Labor (FLSA)
Form I-93 years after hire or 1 year after employment ends, whichever is laterUSCIS
Customer contractsTerm of the contract plus a set period (often 6–7 years)State statute of limitations; ask your lawyer
Card data (full card numbers)Don't store it at all after authorization; let your processor hold itPCI DSS
Security and sign-in logs12 months is a common baselineIndustry practice; some insurers and frameworks ask for it
Backups30–90 days rolling, plus any monthly/yearly copies you choose to keepYour backup policy
Former employee mailboxes and filesSet a period (for example 90 days after departure), then delete or archiveBusiness need; legal hold if a dispute is expected

Regulated businesses have extra rules. Tax preparers and other financial institutions under the FTC Safeguards Rule must dispose of customer information within two years after it was last used, unless there's a business or legal reason to keep it (see the FTC Safeguards Rule checklist). Medical and dental offices should check HIPAA and state medical records laws, which often require keeping patient records for years.

Step 3: Dispose of it properly

  • Paper: cross-cut shredding or a shredding service that gives you a certificate.
  • Computers and drives: wipe or destroy before reuse, sale or recycling. BitLocker-encrypted drives are much safer to dispose of, but still wipe them.
  • Cloud and email: set retention or deletion policies (Microsoft 365 and Google Workspace both have them) instead of relying on people to remember.
  • Legal hold: if a lawsuit, investigation or audit is expected, stop deleting the related records until your lawyer says otherwise.
  • Log it: record what was destroyed, when and by whom, especially for Restricted data.

Common mistakes

  • Keeping old customer exports. "Customers-2019.xlsx" on the server is a breach waiting to happen.
  • Forgetting backups and email. Deleting a file doesn't delete it from backups or from the email you sent it in.
  • No owner. Assign one person to run the yearly cleanup and sign it off.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a small business keep records?

It depends on record type and jurisdiction; tax records are often kept around 7 years. Confirm with your accountant or counsel.

What is a legal hold?

An instruction to preserve records relevant to litigation or investigation, overriding normal deletion schedules.

What are the four data classification levels?

A common scheme is Public, Internal, Confidential and Restricted, with stricter handling at each level. Restricted covers data like card numbers, SSNs and health information.

Do I need to delete customer data?

Keeping data you no longer need increases breach risk, and some rules require disposal. For example, the FTC Safeguards Rule requires covered businesses to dispose of customer information within two years of its last use unless there's a legitimate reason to keep it.

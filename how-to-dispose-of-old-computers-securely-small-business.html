How to dispose of old computers securely: a small business checklist

By William Todd, IT support and cybersecurity engineer · Updated 2026-11-21

Before an old computer, phone, server or copier leaves your business, back up what you need, sign it out of every account, then sanitize the storage: use the drive's built-in secure erase or cryptographic erase, or have it physically destroyed. If you use a recycler, choose one that gives you a certificate of sanitization or destruction listing each serial number, and record it in your disposal log. Deleting files or dragging them to the recycle bin doesn't remove them, and neither does a quick format.

Disposal is where a lot of small-business data leaks happen: a laptop sold online with client files still recoverable, a leased copier returned with years of scanned documents on its hard drive, a phone handed to a family member still signed in to company email. NIST's media sanitization guidelines (SP 800-88, revised in 2025) describe three levels: clear, purge and destroy. For a small business, the practical question is just which of those to use for each device.

Before you wipe anything

  1. Back up and move data the business needs, and confirm the copy opens.
  2. Sign out and remove the device from Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace, your device management (Intune, Jamf, Google endpoint management) and any backup service.
  3. Turn off activation locks: sign out of the Apple ID or Google account on phones, tablets and Macs. Otherwise the next owner can't use it, and you can't prove it was released.
  4. Record the BitLocker or FileVault status and serial number in your asset inventory.
  5. Remove licenses tied to the device so you stop paying for them.

A quick format, deleting files, or "reset this PC" with the default "just remove my files" option are not reliable on their own, especially on SSDs. Full-disk encryption that was on from day one makes disposal much safer, which is one more reason to check BitLocker on every PC.

Choosing a recycler or IT asset disposal vendor

  • Ask for a certificate of sanitization or destruction that lists each device's serial number, the method and the date.
  • Look for recognized certifications for electronics recycling and data destruction (for example R2, e-Stewards or NAID AAA), and check them on the certifier's own site.
  • Ask whether drives are destroyed on site or taken away, and how they're kept secure in transit.
  • Get it in writing that you're not liable for devices once they're collected, and keep the paperwork with your asset records.

Why it matters for compliance

  • FTC Safeguards Rule businesses (tax preparers, mortgage brokers, auto dealers that arrange financing) must securely dispose of customer information (see the checklist).
  • The FTC Disposal Rule requires businesses that use consumer reports, such as background checks, to dispose of that information securely.
  • HIPAA practices must have procedures for disposing of devices and media holding patient data (see the medical and dental checklist).
  • PCI DSS covers secure destruction of cardholder data for businesses that store it.
  • Cyber insurers and state breach laws: an unencrypted device that leaves your control with personal data on it can turn into a reportable breach (see lost laptop steps).

Frequently asked questions

Is formatting a hard drive enough before disposal?

No. A quick format or deleting files leaves data recoverable. Use the drive's secure erase or cryptographic erase, a full overwrite for older spinning drives, or physical destruction.

How do I wipe an SSD before selling a laptop?

Use the drive or computer manufacturer's secure erase or cryptographic erase tool, then reinstall the operating system. If full-disk encryption was on from the start, that adds another layer of protection.

What should a certificate of destruction include?

Each device's serial number, the sanitization or destruction method, the date, and the vendor's details. Keep it with your asset and disposal records.

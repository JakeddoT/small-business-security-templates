Boss gift card scam: how to protect your employees

By William Todd, IT support and cybersecurity engineer · Updated 2026-11-21

If an employee gets an email or text from "the boss" asking them to buy gift cards and send the codes, it's a scam. Tell staff to stop, not reply, and call the real person on a number they already have. If someone has already sent codes, contact the gift card company immediately, keep the cards and receipts, and report it to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov. The FTC's rule of thumb is simple: anyone who asks you to pay with a gift card is a scammer.

Small businesses are a favourite target because staff know the owner, want to help, and often get texts from them. The scammer only needs the owner's name and an employee's email or phone number, both of which are often on your website or LinkedIn.

How the scam usually goes

  1. A message arrives "from" the owner or a manager, often from a personal-looking Gmail address or an unknown mobile number: "Are you at your desk? I need a quick favor."
  2. The request: buy several gift cards for "clients" or "staff appreciation", and it's urgent.
  3. An excuse for not calling: "I'm in a meeting, can't talk, just text me."
  4. A demand to scratch off the backs and send photos or the codes. Once the codes are sent, the money is usually gone within minutes.

Variations ask for a bank transfer, a change to someone's payroll direct deposit, or a payment to a "new vendor". Those are the same trick with bigger amounts (see bank detail change verification).

Red flags to teach staff

  • The sender's address or number isn't the boss's usual one, even if the display name is.
  • Urgency and secrecy: "don't tell anyone, it's a surprise".
  • The boss "can't take a call".
  • Any request to pay with gift cards, crypto or wire transfer outside normal approval.
  • A request to send photos of the back of cards.

Don't blame the employee. People who feel safe reporting a mistake report it in minutes instead of hours, and minutes are what give the card company a chance to act. Log it in your incident log and use it as a training example.

The one-line policy that stops it

We never ask anyone to buy gift cards, send money, or change payment or payroll details by email or text. Any request like that is verified by calling the person on a number we already have, even if it looks like it's from the owner.

Put it in your acceptable use policy, say it out loud at a staff meeting, and have the owner tell staff directly: "I will never ask you for gift cards. If I seem to, it isn't me." That one sentence from the owner does more than any training video.

Make your business a harder target

  • Trim what's public. A staff page with names, roles and email addresses is a gift to scammers. Use a general contact address where you can.
  • Turn on external email warnings so messages from outside the company are clearly marked. Microsoft 365 has a built-in external sender tag for Outlook, and most email security filters can add a warning banner.
  • Set up SPF, DKIM and DMARC on your domain so scammers can't easily send mail that appears to come from your exact address.
  • MFA on email stops the more dangerous version, where the message really comes from the boss's hacked account (MFA rollout plan).
  • Run a 10-minute training session using a real example, and log it (training log). Cyber insurers often ask about phishing training.

Frequently asked questions

Is my boss really asking me to buy gift cards?

Almost certainly not. A request by email or text to buy gift cards and send the codes is a well-known scam. Call your boss on a number you already have before doing anything.

What should I do if I sent gift card codes to a scammer?

Contact the gift card company immediately, tell them the cards were used in a scam and ask for a refund, keep the cards and receipts, stop replying, and report it to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.

How do I stop gift card scams targeting my employees?

Have a clear rule that payment and gift card requests by email or text are always verified by phone, have the owner tell staff they will never ask for gift cards, limit staff details on your website, and turn on external email warnings and MFA.

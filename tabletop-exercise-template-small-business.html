Tabletop exercise for small business: a 60-minute ransomware scenario

By William Todd, IT support and cybersecurity engineer · Updated 2026-11-21

A tabletop exercise is a one-hour guided discussion where your team walks through a pretend incident, stage by stage, and talks through what they'd do. It costs nothing, needs no technical setup, and reveals gaps in your incident response plan before a real attacker does. Insurers and auditors increasingly ask whether you've done one.

Below is a ready-to-run 60-minute ransomware scenario, a shorter bonus scenario on invoice fraud, a facilitator script, and an after-action template.

Who should attend

  • The owner or general manager
  • Whoever handles IT (internal or your IT provider)
  • Office manager or operations lead
  • Someone from finance or billing
  • A facilitator who reads the scenario and keeps time (can be one of the above)

Agenda (60 minutes)

TimeActivity
0–5 minGround rules: no blame, no right answers, talk about what you'd actually do
5–45 minRansomware scenario, five stages, about 8 minutes each
45–55 minDiscussion questions
55–60 minAgree on the top 3 fixes, owners and dates

Scenario: ransomware (read aloud in stages)

  1. 9:05 AM: A staff member says files on the shared drive have strange extensions and won't open. What do you do in the first 10 minutes? Who do they tell?
  2. 9:20 AM: Two more computers show a ransom note demanding payment within 48 hours. Who do you call? Is the network disconnected? Are computers turned off or left on?
  3. 10:00 AM: Your IT provider confirms ransomware. Backups are your only recovery path. When were they last tested? Were they connected to the network? Who has the backup console password?
  4. Noon: Customers are calling because you can't take orders or see appointments. What do you tell them? Who speaks for the business? Can you work on paper?
  5. Next day: The attackers say they copied customer data and will publish it. Who decides whether notification is required? Do you have your cyber insurer's breach hotline and a lawyer's number on paper?

Compare the answers with the real steps in the ransomware first-hour guide.

Facilitator tips

  • Reveal one stage at a time; don't hand out the whole scenario.
  • Ask "how would you actually do that?" Find the phone number; find the backup login. Gaps hide in the details.
  • Write every "we don't know" on a whiteboard. That list is the output.
  • Keep it moving. If a stage gets stuck, note the gap and go on.

Bonus 20-minute scenario: invoice fraud

  1. Your regular supplier emails saying they've changed banks and attaching an updated invoice. Accounts payable is about to pay. What's the process?
  2. The payment went out yesterday. The supplier calls asking why they haven't been paid. What do you do in the first hour? (Answer: see bank-change fraud steps.)
  3. Investigation shows it was your mailbox that was hacked, and the attacker also emailed your customers with your bank details changed. Who do you call first?

Discussion questions

  • Did everyone know who the incident lead was?
  • Did we know how to reach our IT provider, insurer and lawyer after hours, without email?
  • Could we operate manually for a day? A week?
  • Would our backups actually survive this? When was the last restore test?
  • What is the one thing we'll fix this month?

Frequently asked questions

What is a tabletop exercise in cybersecurity?

A discussion-based session where a team talks through how they would respond to a simulated incident, to find gaps in plans and roles.

How often should a small business run a tabletop exercise?

At least once a year, and after major changes to staff, systems, or your incident response plan.

How long should a tabletop exercise take?

For a small business, 45 to 90 minutes is usually enough.

How long does a tabletop exercise take?

About an hour for a small business: five minutes of ground rules, 40 minutes walking through the scenario in stages, and 15 minutes on discussion and agreeing fixes.

What scenario should a small business use for a tabletop exercise?

Ransomware is the most common choice because it tests backups, contacts and communication. Invoice or payment fraud is a good second scenario.

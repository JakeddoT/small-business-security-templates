IT asset inventory template for small business: what to track for security

By William Todd, IT support and cybersecurity engineer · Updated 2026-11-21

A small business IT asset inventory should list every device that touches company data (laptops, desktops, phones, servers, network gear and card terminals) with its owner, serial number, location, operating system and support status, encryption status, and warranty or replacement date. Add a second list of cloud accounts and software, with who administers each one. That's the inventory a cyber insurer, an auditor or the FTC Safeguards Rule expects you to be able to produce, and the one you'll need the day a laptop goes missing.

Most asset inventory templates focus on accounting: purchase price and depreciation. For security, the fields that matter are different. A spreadsheet is fine for 5–50 users; the hard part is keeping it current.

The accounts and software inventory

Devices are half the picture. Most small-business data now lives in cloud accounts, and those are what attackers go after. List each one:

Domain registrars and social media accounts are the ones most often registered to a former employee's personal email. Check who actually owns each login, and fix it before they leave (see the offboarding checklist).

Collecting the details from Windows PCs

On each Windows PC, these built-in PowerShell commands show the fields you need. Run them in PowerShell; the BitLocker one needs an administrator window.

  • Make and model: Get-CimInstance Win32_ComputerSystem | Select Manufacturer,Model
  • Serial number: Get-CimInstance Win32_BIOS | Select SerialNumber
  • Windows version: Get-CimInstance Win32_OperatingSystem | Select Caption,Version
  • Encryption: Get-BitLockerVolume -MountPoint C: | Select ProtectionStatus

If you have an IT provider with remote management (RMM) software, they can usually export most of this for every device at once. Ask for it as a spreadsheet each quarter. For a deeper per-PC check, see the Windows security audit with PowerShell.

Keeping it current

  • Update it at the moments that change it: new hire, leaver, new device, repair, disposal.
  • Quarterly check: compare the list against what your management tools, Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace device lists show. Anything in one list and not the other needs explaining.
  • Disposal: record the date and method when a device is wiped or destroyed. Insurers and regulators may ask how you dispose of devices that held customer data.
  • Keep it somewhere safe. The inventory is a map of your business; don't leave it in a public share.

Who asks for an inventory

  • Cyber insurers ask about device counts, encryption, EDR and unsupported systems (see the insurance questionnaire guide).
  • The FTC Safeguards Rule lists a data and systems inventory among the required safeguards for covered businesses (see the checklist).
  • Your IT provider needs it to quote, support and plan replacements (see the MSP onboarding checklist).

Frequently asked questions

What should a small business IT asset inventory include?

Every device that touches company data with its ID, type, serial number, assigned user, location, operating system, encryption and antivirus status, and warranty or replacement date, plus a list of cloud accounts with their admins and MFA status.

How often should I update an IT asset inventory?

Whenever someone joins or leaves, or a device is bought, repaired or disposed of, with a quarterly check against your management tools to catch anything missed.

Is a spreadsheet good enough for an IT asset inventory?

For most businesses with up to about 50 users, yes, as long as it includes the security fields and is kept current. Larger fleets usually benefit from asset management or RMM software.

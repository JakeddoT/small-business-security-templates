Phishing quiz: can your team spot the scam?

By William Todd, IT support and cybersecurity engineer · Updated 2026-11-22

Can you spot the scam? Below are eight short, realistic messages of the kind small-business staff receive. Decide whether each is a scam or looks legitimate, and you'll see why straight away. It takes about three minutes, works on a phone, and nothing is stored or sent anywhere. Use it at a team meeting as a quick training exercise, then log the session.

The quiz

1. Email from "Microsoft 365" <no-reply@microsoft365-alerts.example>: Your mailbox is full. Sign in within 24 hours to keep receiving email. Button: Sign in now.

Scam. The sender's domain isn't Microsoft's, and urgency plus a sign-in button is the classic fake login page. Go to your mailbox the usual way instead.

2. Text from an unknown number: Hi, it's Sam (the owner). New number. Are you at your desk? I need you to grab some gift cards for a client, I'll pay you back.

Scam. A new number, urgency, secrecy and gift cards are the boss impersonation pattern. Call the owner on the number you already have.

3. Your accounting software shows its usual in-app banner: An update is available. Restart to install. You opened the app yourself.

Probably legitimate. You started the session, it's inside the app you opened, and it doesn't ask for a password or payment. If in doubt, ask IT.

4. Email from your regular supplier's real address: We've changed banks. Please use the attached details for this month's invoice.

Treat as fraud until verified. The address can be real if their mailbox was hacked. Call the supplier on a number you already have before changing anything.

5. A PDF attachment from an unknown sender contains only a QR code: Scan to review your updated benefits.

Scam. A QR code in an unexpected PDF is a common way to get you onto a fake login page on your phone. Never scan a QR code to sign in.

6. You get an MFA prompt on your phone at 7 a.m. while you're still at home and haven't tried to sign in.

Deny it and report it. An MFA prompt you didn't start means someone may have your password. Change it from a trusted device.

7. A calendar invite from your manager's real account for the weekly team meeting, at the usual time, with no links except the normal Teams join button.

Probably legitimate: expected, from a known account, consistent with routine. Still, if the meeting link asks you to sign in again, stop and check.

8. A Teams chat from "IT Help Desk (External)": We saw spam flooding your inbox. Open Quick Assist and read me the code so I can fix it.

Scam. Unexpected IT contact from an external account asking for remote access is a known ransomware tactic. Close the chat and call IT on the number you have.

The rules behind the answers

  • Check the real sender address and domain, not the display name (see how to check if an email is spoofed).
  • Never sign in from a link, QR code or text you didn't expect; go to the site or app the normal way (see QR code phishing).
  • Never approve or share an MFA code you didn't ask for.
  • Bank detail changes and gift card requests are verified by phone on a known number, even when the email is from a real account (see bank detail change verification and the gift card scam).
  • Unexpected IT contact asking for remote access is a scam until proven otherwise (see fake IT support calls).

  2. Give everyone three minutes, then discuss the ones people got wrong.

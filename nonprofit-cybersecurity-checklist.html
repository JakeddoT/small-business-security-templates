Nonprofit cybersecurity checklist for small organizations

By William Todd, IT support and cybersecurity engineer · Updated 2026-11-21

For a small nonprofit, charity or church, the cybersecurity basics are: MFA on email, the donation platform and the bank; a password manager instead of shared passwords passed between volunteers; organization-owned accounts rather than personal ones; removing access when staff, volunteers or board members leave; a call-back rule for any payment or bank detail change; tested backups of donor and financial records; and a short incident plan. Most of it is free, and many technology companies offer nonprofit discounts or donated licenses.

Nonprofits are targeted for the same reasons as small businesses, with a few extras: lots of people come and go, accounts are often in a volunteer's personal name, donor data is valuable, and scammers know that a friendly request from "the pastor" or "the board chair" gets a fast response.

Volunteers and shared computers

  • Give each regular volunteer their own login where the system allows; avoid one shared "office" account.
  • Only give volunteers access to what their role needs. A volunteer posting on social media doesn't need the donor database.
  • On a shared office computer, use separate user accounts and sign out of email and banking after each use.
  • Keep a simple list of who has access to what, and review it when roles change (see the asset inventory).

Scams that target nonprofits

  • Leader impersonation: an email or text that appears to come from the director, pastor or board chair asking for gift cards or a quick payment.
  • Fake grant or vendor bank changes: a request to send a grant or invoice payment to a new account (see the deepfake and impersonation verification rules).
  • Overpayment donations: a large "donation" by check or card followed by a request to refund part of it. Wait until funds have truly cleared and refund only to the original payment method.
  • Fake donation pages that copy your name during an appeal. Tell supporters the one official place to give.

Board oversight

Boards don't need technical detail, but they should know that the basics above are in place. A short annual report from whoever manages IT covering MFA, backups, account ownership and any incidents is usually enough. If you have cyber insurance, check what the insurer requires (see the cyber insurance questionnaire guide).

Low-cost help

Many large technology providers run nonprofit programs with discounted or donated software, including office suites and security tools. Check eligibility on each provider's own nonprofit page. Local volunteer IT groups and your national nonprofit association may also offer guidance.

Frequently asked questions

What cybersecurity does a small nonprofit need?

MFA on email, banking and donation platforms, a password manager instead of shared passwords, organization-owned accounts, prompt removal of access when people leave, a call-back rule for payment changes, tested backups and a short incident plan.

How should nonprofits manage volunteer access?

Give regular volunteers their own logins with only the access their role needs, avoid shared office accounts, keep a list of who has access to what, and remove access when they step down.

What scams target churches and charities?

Emails or texts impersonating the pastor or director asking for gift cards, fake bank detail changes for grants or vendors, overpayment donation refunds and fake donation pages.

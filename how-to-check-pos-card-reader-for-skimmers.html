How to check a POS card reader for skimmers: a 2-minute daily routine

By William Todd, IT support and cybersecurity engineer · Updated 2026-11-21

At the start of every shift, compare each card reader to a reference photo, tug the card slot and keypad gently, check the cables and seals, and confirm the serial number matches your inventory. It takes about two minutes per register, and it's one of the most effective defenses a small store or restaurant has against card skimming.

Skimmers on in-store terminals are usually thin overlays on the card slot or keypad, a hidden camera aimed at the PIN pad, or a whole terminal swapped for a tampered one, often during a "technician visit". Most can be spotted by someone who knows what the device normally looks like.

The 2-minute check

  1. Know what normal looks like. Keep photos of each reader and PIN pad from the front, sides, back and underneath, printed or on a phone at the register.
  2. Check the card slot and tap area. Look for anything loose, bulky, glued, scratched or a slightly different color or texture. Gently tug the slot; overlays often shift.
  3. Check the PIN pad. Spongy or raised keys, a keypad that sits higher than usual, or tiny holes or cameras above the keypad or in nearby displays.
  4. Check cables and seals. Look for new or extra cables, devices plugged in between the reader and the register, or broken or replaced tamper seals and stickers.
  5. Match the serial number on the back or underneath to your inventory. Swapping a whole device is a known attack.
  6. Verify visitors. Never let anyone service, replace or "update" a terminal without confirming with your manager and the POS provider on a number you already have.
  7. If something is wrong, stop using the terminal, don't remove anything, photograph it, and call your manager and your payment processor.

Sample inspection log

Log each check with the date, time and initials. A simple log shows your processor and insurer that you inspect regularly, which matters if you ever have a breach.

Set it up once

  • Inventory every terminal: make, model, serial number, location and who's responsible. Include handhelds and backup units in the drawer.
  • Take reference photos of each device and keep them with the log.
  • Add tamper-evident seals on seams and cable connections if your processor doesn't provide them, and record their numbers.
  • Secure the devices: mount or tether terminals where you can, and lock spares away overnight.
  • Train staff: show everyone what a skimmer looks like and the visitor-verification rule. Record it in your training log.

Social engineering to watch for

  • A "technician" arrives unannounced to replace or upgrade a terminal.
  • A caller claiming to be the processor asks staff to run a test transaction, read out terminal details or install software.
  • Someone asks to plug a device into the register "to check the connection".

The answer is always the same: verify with your manager and the provider on a known number first.

Frequently asked questions

How often should POS terminals be checked for skimmers?

At the start of every shift, or at least daily, with a more thorough weekly inspection.

What should I do if I find a skimmer?

Stop using the terminal, do not remove the device yourself, document it, and contact your manager, payment processor, and local police.

How often should I check card readers for skimmers?

A quick check at the start of every shift is a common practice, with a more thorough inspection weekly. Log each check.

What does a card skimmer look like on a POS terminal?

Often a thin overlay on the card slot or keypad that looks slightly bulky or loose, a hidden camera above the PIN pad, extra cables, or a whole terminal swapped for a tampered one.

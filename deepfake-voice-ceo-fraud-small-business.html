Deepfake voice and video scams: verification rules for small businesses

By William Todd, IT support and cybersecurity engineer · Updated 2026-11-21

Criminals can now use AI to imitate a real person's voice, and sometimes their face on a video call, well enough to fool colleagues. In a small business the target is usually a payment, a gift card purchase, a payroll or bank detail change, or a password reset requested "by the owner". The defense isn't spotting the fake: it's a rule that money and access requests are always confirmed through a second channel you start yourself, such as calling the person back on the number you already have, plus a second approver for payments.

The FBI has warned that criminals use generative AI, including voice cloning, to make fraud more convincing, and that AI-generated voices can sound almost identical to the real person. Short recordings from a voicemail greeting, a video or a podcast can be enough raw material, so assume anyone's voice can be faked.

What deepfake requests look like

ScenarioTypical ask
A call or voicemail from "the owner", who's travelingWire money to a new supplier today, or buy gift cards
A video call with "the manager" whose camera is glitchyApprove an urgent payment or share a file
A voice note on WhatsApp or text from "the boss" on a new numberKeep this confidential; just do it
A call from "an employee" to the IT providerReset my password and MFA, I've lost my phone
A call from "a regular supplier"Our bank details changed, use these from now on

The pattern is the same as in older scams: urgency, secrecy, a new channel or number, and a request to move money or grant access. Only the voice is new (see the boss gift card scam and direct deposit scam).

The verification rules

  1. Call back on a known number. Any request involving money, bank details, passwords, MFA or sensitive data is confirmed by hanging up and calling the person on a number from your contacts or records, never the number that called you or one given in the message.
  2. Two people for payments. New payees, changed bank details and payments above an amount you set need a second approver (see bank detail change verification).
  3. No exceptions for the boss. The owner agrees in writing that staff will always verify, even if it's really them and they're in a hurry. This is what makes the rule work.
  4. Optional code phrase. Some businesses agree a verbal phrase for urgent requests, similar to the family code word the FBI suggests for personal scams. Treat it as an extra check, not a replacement for calling back, and change it if it might be known.
  5. Help desk identity checks. Your IT provider shouldn't reset passwords or MFA on the strength of a voice alone. Agree a check, such as a call back to the number on file or confirmation from a manager.

A rule to give staff

If anyone, even someone who sounds exactly like the owner, asks you by phone, voice message or video to send money, buy gift cards, change bank details or share passwords or codes, stop. Hang up and call them back on the number we have on file, and get a second approval for any payment. You'll never be in trouble for checking.

Add it to your acceptable use policy, cover it in training and record it in your training log.

Reduce what scammers can copy

  • Consider a generic voicemail greeting for the owner and finance staff.
  • Be aware that public videos and podcasts give scammers voice samples; you don't need to stop, just make sure the verification rules are in place.
  • Keep the list of who approves payments out of public view, and don't post travel plans for the owner or finance staff in real time.

If money or access was already given

  1. Call your bank's fraud line immediately to try to stop or recall the payment, and file a complaint at ic3.gov.
  2. If a password or MFA was reset, secure the account straight away (see the Microsoft 365 hacked account checklist).
  3. Keep the evidence: call logs, voicemails, messages and the numbers used.
  4. Tell your cyber insurer, since many policies have conditions on social engineering fraud cover, and record it in your incident log.

Frequently asked questions

Can scammers really clone a boss's voice?

Yes. AI tools can imitate a person's voice from recorded audio, and the FBI has warned that cloned voices can sound nearly identical to the real person. Assume any voice can be faked.

How do I verify a call from my boss asking for money?

Hang up and call them back on the number you already have, not the number that called you, and get a second approval for the payment. Make this a rule that applies even to the owner.

Should my business use a code word against deepfakes?

It can help as an extra check for urgent requests, but it shouldn't replace calling back on a known number and a second approver for payments.

Direct deposit change scam: how to stop payroll diversion

By William Todd, IT support and cybersecurity engineer · Updated 2026-11-21

Treat every request to change an employee's direct deposit details as unverified until you've confirmed it with the employee in person or on a phone number already on file, never one from the request itself. In payroll diversion scams, criminals pose as an employee (or take over their email or payroll login) and ask for pay to go to a new account. The next paycheck goes to the scammer, and the real employee finds out on payday.

The FBI has warned about payroll diversion since at least 2018. It hits small businesses hard because the person handling payroll often knows the employee, the request looks routine, and there's no second check. The fix is a simple verification rule that everyone follows every time.

How the scam works

  • The fake employee email. HR or the owner gets a message such as "I've changed banks, can you update my direct deposit before the next payroll? New details attached." It often comes from a personal address with the employee's name as the display name, or from a lookalike address.
  • The hacked employee account. The scammer phishes the employee's email or payroll portal login, changes the bank details themselves, and sets up email rules so the employee doesn't see the "your deposit details changed" notification.
  • The timing. Requests usually arrive just before a payroll cutoff, so there's pressure to "just do it".

The diverted pay is often sent to a prepaid card account or a newly opened account controlled by the criminals, and moved out quickly.

Red flags

  • A deposit change request by email, especially from a non-company address
  • Urgency: "before this Friday's payroll"
  • The employee is hard to reach, or "traveling"
  • A new bank the business has never seen for that person, or a prepaid card or online-only bank
  • Several employees' details changed in the payroll portal within a short time
  • Logins to the payroll portal at odd hours or from unusual locations

A verification process that stops it

  1. No changes by email alone. Direct deposit changes are made only by the employee in the payroll portal (protected with MFA), or on a signed form handed over in person.
  2. Call back on a known number. For any change, call the employee on the number in your HR records, not one in the email. Or confirm face to face.
  3. Notify both accounts. When details change, send a confirmation to the employee's work email and tell them in person or by phone.
  4. Hold period. Apply changes from the next payroll after verification, not the one already being processed, unless confirmed in person.
  5. Log it. Record who requested, who verified, how, and when.

The same rule protects you from fake vendors asking you to pay a new account (see vendor bank detail change verification).

Lock down the payroll portal

  • Require MFA for every employee and admin login to the payroll or HR portal. Most providers support it; turn it on for everyone (MFA rollout plan).
  • Use unique passwords for payroll; the FBI specifically advises that payroll credentials shouldn't be reused elsewhere.
  • Turn on change alerts to the employee and to payroll staff whenever bank details change.
  • Check for email forwarding and inbox rules on any employee account involved, since attackers hide the alert emails this way (see Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace cleanup steps).

If a paycheck was already diverted

  1. Call your bank and payroll provider immediately. Ask whether the payment can be stopped or recalled. Speed matters most here.
  2. Secure the accounts: reset the employee's email and payroll passwords, revoke sessions, turn on MFA, and remove any suspicious inbox rules.
  3. Correct the bank details after verifying in person.
  4. Report it to the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center at ic3.gov (mention payroll diversion in the complaint) and to local police.
  5. Tell your cyber insurer if you have a policy. Some cover social engineering losses, some don't, and most require prompt notice.
  6. Check other employees' payroll details for recent changes.
  7. Get advice on paying the affected employee. Wage obligations vary by state, so ask your payroll provider or employment lawyer rather than guessing.

Record it in your incident log and use it in your next staff training. A related scam targets staff with fake requests from the owner; see the boss gift card scam.

Frequently asked questions

What is payroll diversion fraud?

A scam where criminals pose as an employee, or take over their email or payroll login, and change the direct deposit details so the next paycheck goes to an account they control.

How should HR verify a direct deposit change?

Confirm with the employee in person or by calling the phone number already in your HR records, never a number or link from the request, and have changes made through an MFA-protected payroll portal.

What should I do if an employee's paycheck was diverted?

Call your bank and payroll provider immediately to try to stop or recall the payment, secure the employee's email and payroll accounts, report it at ic3.gov, notify your cyber insurer, and check other employees' details.

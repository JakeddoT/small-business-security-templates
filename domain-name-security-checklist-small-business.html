Domain name security checklist for small business

By William Todd, IT support and cybersecurity engineer · Updated 2026-11-21

To protect your business domain name: make sure the business owns the registrar account (not a former employee or web designer), turn on MFA for it, turn on auto-renew with a payment card that won't expire unnoticed, keep the transfer lock on, use a contact email that isn't on the same domain, and limit who can change DNS. Your domain runs your email and website, so losing it, to an expired renewal or to someone who takes over the registrar account, can stop the business and let an attacker receive your email.

Why the domain matters more than it looks

  • Email depends on it. Whoever controls the domain's DNS controls where your email is delivered, and can reset passwords for any account that uses that email.
  • Expiry is the most common failure. The renewal card expired, the reminder went to an old employee's mailbox, and the website and email stop working.
  • Hijacking usually starts with the registrar login. A reused password and no MFA on the registrar account is all it takes.

If your domain is about to expire or has expired

Renew it immediately from the registrar account. Registrars of generic domains such as .com are required by ICANN policy to send renewal reminders before expiry, and many registrars give a grace period after expiry in which you can still renew, though your website and email may stop working during it. After that, many generic domains go into a redemption period, typically around 30 days, where recovery is still possible but usually costs more. Country-code domains (for example .uk or .ca) follow their own rules. Don't wait: once a domain is released, someone else can register it.

If you think someone has taken over your domain

  1. Contact your registrar's support at once, using the phone number or support page from their real website, and ask them to lock the domain.
  2. Secure the registrar account: reset the password, check MFA methods, remove unknown users and API keys.
  3. Check DNS records against your documented list, especially MX (email), the website address and any new records.
  4. Check email: because an attacker who changed DNS may have received your mail, reset passwords on important accounts that use your domain email and follow your incident response plan.
  5. Tell your bank and key customers to be wary of any message asking to change payment details (see bank detail change verification).
  6. Tell your cyber insurer if you have a policy, since many require prompt notice.

When people leave

If a staff member, web designer or IT provider who had registrar access leaves, remove their access and change any shared password the same day, and confirm the account email and recovery phone number don't belong to them (see the offboarding checklist). If an agency registered your domain in their own account, ask them in writing to move it into an account the business owns.

Extra protection for important domains

  • Registry lock: some registrars offer a higher-level lock, usually for a fee, where changes need a manual verification step. Worth considering if your domain handles payments or lots of customer email.
  • Register obvious look-alikes (common misspellings or other extensions) if impersonation is a real risk for your business.
  • DNSSEC can help protect DNS answers from tampering; ask your registrar or DNS host whether it's supported and simple to turn on.

Frequently asked questions

How do I protect my business domain name?

Make sure the business owns the registrar account, turn on MFA, keep auto-renew on with a current card, keep the transfer lock on, use a contact email on a different domain, and turn on change alerts.

What happens if my business domain expires?

Your website and email can stop working. Many registrars allow renewal for a period after expiry, and generic domains often have a redemption period of about 30 days at a higher cost, but once released anyone can register it.

What is a domain transfer lock?

A registrar setting that prevents your domain from being moved to another registrar until you unlock it from your account. Keep it on unless you're deliberately transferring.

Tax preparer data breach: who to contact and what to do first

By William Todd, IT support and cybersecurity engineer · Updated 2026-11-21

If client data at your tax practice may have been stolen: contain it, then contact your local IRS Stakeholder Liaison as quickly as possible. The IRS says that when it's told quickly, it can take steps to block fraudulent returns filed in your clients' names. After that, notify the states where you prepare returns, law enforcement, your cyber insurer, and (if 500 or more people are affected) the FTC.

Signs that send preparers here: clients' e-filed returns rejected because a return was already filed, IRS letters to clients about returns they didn't file, more e-file acknowledgments than returns you sent, or computers acting on their own. This is general information, not legal advice. The IRS's Data Theft Information for Tax Professionals page and Publication 5293 have the current official steps.

First hour: contain

  1. Disconnect affected computers from the network, but leave them on. Don't wipe or reinstall anything.
  2. Change passwords for email, tax software, e-Services and cloud storage from a clean device, and sign out of all sessions. Turn on MFA everywhere it isn't already.
  3. Call your IT provider and your cyber insurer's breach hotline. Many policies require prompt notice and provide forensic and legal help.
  4. Start an incident log: what you noticed, when, which systems, what you've done. See the incident response plan template.

Who to contact

Don't use the general IRS toll-free numbers for this; they can't take third-party reports of data theft. Find your Stakeholder Liaison through the IRS website.

Helping affected clients

  • Tell clients what happened and what you're doing, once your lawyer agrees on the wording and timing.
  • Point them to an IRS Identity Protection PIN (IP PIN), which helps prevent someone else filing using their SSN.
  • If a fraudulent return was filed in their name, they may need to file Form 14039 (Identity Theft Affidavit), as the IRS instructs.
  • Suggest credit freezes with the three credit bureaus.

After the dust settles

  • Find the cause with your IT provider or the insurer's forensic firm: phishing, stolen password, remote access, malware. Fix it before you file again.
  • Update your WISP and risk assessment with what you learned (see the WISP guide for tax preparers).
  • Report to your Qualified Individual's annual report under the FTC Safeguards Rule (see Qualified Individual).
  • Train staff on what happened, without blame, and log it.

The controls that prevent most practice breaches

  • MFA on email, tax software, cloud storage and remote access (the IRS requires MFA for some tax software and e-Services access).
  • Phishing training, especially "new client" emails with malicious attachments during filing season.
  • Encrypted laptops and backups, with one backup offline (restore test).
  • No exposed remote desktop (check).
  • A written incident plan with the Stakeholder Liaison's contact details filled in before you need them.

Frequently asked questions

What should a tax preparer do after a data breach?

Contain it by disconnecting affected computers and changing passwords, then contact your local IRS Stakeholder Liaison quickly, notify state tax agencies, law enforcement and your cyber insurer, and check FTC and state notification duties.

Who do tax professionals report data theft to?

Their local IRS Stakeholder Liaison first, plus state tax agencies where they prepare returns, the FBI and local police, their cyber insurer, and the FTC if the Safeguards Rule notification threshold of 500 consumers is met.

Do tax preparers have to notify the FTC of a breach?

Under the FTC Safeguards Rule, covered businesses must notify the FTC of a notification event involving the unencrypted information of at least 500 consumers, as soon as possible and no later than 30 days after discovery.

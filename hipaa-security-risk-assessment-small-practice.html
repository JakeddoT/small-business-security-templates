Security risk assessment for small medical and dental practices: how to organize it

By William Todd, IT support and cybersecurity engineer · Updated 2026-11-21

A HIPAA security risk assessment is a documented review of where your practice's electronic patient information (ePHI) lives, what could go wrong with it, how likely and how bad each risk is, and what you'll do about it. The HIPAA Security Rule requires covered entities, including small medical and dental practices, to do one (45 CFR 164.308(a)(1)(ii)(A)) and to keep it current. Not having one is among the most common findings in HHS enforcement actions.

For a small practice, the assessment doesn't have to be a 100-page report. It has to be honest, specific to your practice, written down, and acted on. Below is a practical way to organize it. A qualified professional can confirm it meets your obligations. HHS also offers a free Security Risk Assessment (SRA) Tool designed for small and medium practices.

Step 1: Inventory where ePHI lives

List every place patient information is created, stored, sent or viewed. Most practices miss at least three of these:

  • EHR and practice management system (cloud or on a local server)
  • Email, including staff sending X-rays or referrals as attachments
  • File shares and cloud storage (scanned forms, spreadsheets, exports)
  • Front-desk PCs, laptops and phones, including any personal phones used for messaging
  • Imaging systems (X-ray sensors, intraoral cameras, ultrasound) and their PCs
  • Fax, scanners, multifunction printers (many store copies on an internal drive)
  • Backups, both local and cloud
  • Vendors: billing service, IT provider, EHR vendor, answering service, labs. Each needs a business associate agreement (BAA)
  • Paper records and where they're stored and shredded

Step 2: Identify threats and rate them

For each system, ask what could realistically go wrong. Rate likelihood and impact as Low / Medium / High, and combine them into a risk level.

ThreatExampleLikelihoodImpactRisk
Phishing / email takeoverFront-desk mailbox compromised, used to send fake invoicesHighHighHigh
RansomwareServer and imaging PCs encrypted; can't see patientsMediumHighHigh
Lost or stolen deviceUnencrypted laptop with exported patient listMediumHighHigh
Unauthorized staff accessShared login lets anyone view any chartMediumMediumMedium
Vendor breachBilling company exposes recordsLowHighMedium
Improper disposalOld PC or printer drive recycled without wipingLowMediumLow

Note the controls you already have (for example "MFA on email: yes; laptops encrypted: 2 of 3"). The assessment is about the gap between the risk and your current controls.

Step 3: Write the remediation plan

For each Medium and High risk, list the action, an owner and a due date:

RiskActionOwnerDue
Email takeoverEnforce MFA for all users; block external auto-forwardingIT provider30 days
RansomwareAdd an offline or immutable backup; test a restoreIT provider60 days
Lost laptopEncrypt the remaining laptop; remove local exportsOffice manager14 days
Shared loginUnique EHR logins with role-based accessOffice manager30 days
Vendor breachCollect missing BAAs; review vendor list yearlyOffice manager45 days

Step 4: Document and repeat

  • Date and sign the assessment, and keep it with your HIPAA records. HIPAA documentation is generally kept for six years.
  • Repeat it at least yearly, and whenever something major changes: a new EHR, a move, a new location, remote work, or a security incident.
  • Track the remediation plan to completion. An assessment that lists risks you never fix can look worse than none.

Common mistakes

  • Using a generic template unchanged. It must describe your systems and risks.
  • Leaving out imaging and devices. X-ray and imaging PCs often run old Windows versions and are rarely patched.
  • Assuming the EHR vendor covers it. A cloud EHR shifts some risk to the vendor, but your email, devices, staff and paper are still yours.
  • No BAAs. Every vendor that handles ePHI needs one.

HHS proposed significant updates to the HIPAA Security Rule in 2025. Check the current status with your compliance advisor before relying on any specific requirement. Frequently asked questions

How often should a small practice do a risk assessment?

At least annually and after significant changes such as a new EHR, office move, or security incident.

Is a security risk assessment required under HIPAA?

Yes. The HIPAA Security Rule requires covered entities and business associates to conduct an accurate and thorough risk analysis of risks to electronic protected health information (45 CFR 164.308(a)(1)(ii)(A)), and to keep it current.

How often should a small practice do a HIPAA risk assessment?

At least yearly is the common practice, and again whenever there's a major change, such as a new EHR, a new office, remote work or a security incident.

Is there a free HIPAA risk assessment tool?

Yes. HHS and ONC offer a free Security Risk Assessment (SRA) Tool aimed at small and medium-sized practices.

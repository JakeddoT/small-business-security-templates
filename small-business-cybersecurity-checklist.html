Small business cybersecurity checklist: 10 controls insurers and clients ask about

By William Todd, IT support and cybersecurity engineer · Updated 2026-11-21

The ten security controls that come up in almost every cyber insurance, client and payment-processor questionnaire are: MFA on email, MFA on remote access and admin accounts, endpoint protection, restorable backups, patching, phishing training, an incident response plan, least privilege, clean offboarding and vendor access control. If you can show evidence for each, you're ahead of most small businesses.

Sooner or later someone will ask how you protect your systems: an insurer at renewal, a large client in a vendor questionnaire, or your payment processor. Use this checklist to find your gaps, with the evidence that proves each control is in place.

The 10-control checklist

7Written incident response planWho to call, how to contain, how to preserve evidence; reviewed yearlyIR plan + tabletop notes
8Least privilegePeople get the access their job needs; staff aren't local admins; admin accounts reviewed quarterlyAdmin account review record
9Clean offboardingAccounts disabled, sessions revoked, shared passwords reset, devices recovered on the last dayOffboarding checklist records

Where to start if you have none of these

Turn on MFA for email first, then confirm your backups actually restore. Those two controls prevent or limit the most common small business incidents: account takeover (and the invoice fraud that follows) and ransomware. Then work down the list:

  1. Week 1: MFA on email and admin accounts (rollout plan).
  2. Week 2: test a backup restore; make sure one copy is offline or immutable.
  3. Week 3: check endpoint protection and patch status on every PC; remove staff from local admin.
  4. Week 4: close exposed remote access (RDP check) and put MFA on the VPN.
  5. Month 2: write the incident response plan, run phishing training, and set up the offboarding checklist.
  6. Month 3: list vendors with access, and write the core policies (acceptable use, password and MFA).

Two more worth adding

  • Payment verification: a callback rule before paying new bank details stops the most expensive small-business fraud (procedure).
  • Disk encryption on laptops, so a lost device isn't a data breach (BitLocker check).

Industry add-ons

  • Retail and restaurants: PCI checklist and daily skimmer checks.
  • Medical and dental: practice checklist and a yearly HIPAA risk assessment.
  • Tax preparers and accountants: a WISP under the FTC Safeguards Rule.

Frequently asked questions

What cybersecurity controls do insurers require for small businesses?

Most insurers ask about MFA on email, remote access and admin accounts, endpoint protection, offline or immutable backups, patching, phishing training, and a written incident response plan.

What is the first security step a small business should take?

Enable multi-factor authentication on every email account, then verify that backups can be restored.

Do small businesses need written security policies?

Increasingly yes. Insurers, larger clients, and auditors often ask for written policies as evidence that controls exist.

What cybersecurity controls do small businesses need?

The core ten: MFA on email, MFA on remote access and admin accounts, endpoint protection, restorable backups, regular patching, phishing training, an incident response plan, least privilege, clean offboarding and vendor access control.

Where should a small business start with cybersecurity?

Turn on MFA for email and confirm your backups actually restore. Those two controls address the most common small-business incidents: account takeover and ransomware.

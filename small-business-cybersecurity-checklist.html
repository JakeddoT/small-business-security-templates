Small business cybersecurity checklist: 10 controls insurers and clients ask about

By William Todd, IT support and cybersecurity engineer · Updated 2026-11-21

Sooner or later a cyber insurer, a large client, or your payment processor will ask how you protect your systems. These ten controls come up in almost every questionnaire. If you can show evidence for each, you are ahead of most small businesses.

  1. MFA on email. Email is the most common way attackers get in. Use an authenticator app rather than SMS where possible.
  2. MFA on remote access and admin accounts. VPN, remote desktop, cloud admin consoles, and every administrator login.
  3. Endpoint protection everywhere. Current antivirus or EDR on every laptop, desktop, and server, with someone reviewing alerts.
  4. Backups you can restore. Keep one copy offline or immutable and test a restore at least yearly.
  5. Patching on a schedule. Security updates within 30 days, critical ones faster. Remove software that no longer gets updates.
  6. Phishing awareness training. Teach staff to report suspicious messages and to never approve an MFA prompt they did not start.
  7. A written incident response plan. Who to call, how to contain, how to preserve evidence.
  8. Least privilege. People get the access their job needs. Review admin accounts every quarter.
  9. Clean offboarding. Disable accounts, revoke sessions, reset shared passwords, and recover devices on the last day.
  10. Vendor access control. Know which vendors can reach your systems, and require MFA and named accounts.

Where to start if you have none of these

Turn on MFA for email first, then confirm your backups actually restore. Those two controls prevent or limit the most common small business incidents: account takeover and ransomware.

Frequently asked questions

What cybersecurity controls do insurers require for small businesses?

Most insurers ask about MFA on email, remote access and admin accounts, endpoint protection, offline or immutable backups, patching, phishing training, and a written incident response plan.

What is the first security step a small business should take?

Enable multi-factor authentication on every email account, then verify that backups can be restored.

Do small businesses need written security policies?

Increasingly yes. Insurers, larger clients, and auditors often ask for written policies as evidence that controls exist.

Security awareness training log template

By William Todd, IT support and cybersecurity engineer · Updated 2026-11-22

A security awareness training log is a dated record of who was trained, on what, when, how, and when they're next due. Cyber insurance applications, client security questionnaires, HIPAA and the FTC Safeguards Rule all ask the same thing: can you prove your staff were trained? Running training is only half of it. Without the record, it didn't happen as far as an insurer or auditor is concerned.

Below: what to record, a sample log, a minimum program for a small business, a ready-made topic plan for the year and a 30-minute session outline you can run yourself.

What to record for every session

  • Date and topic (for example, "Phishing and invoice fraud, 30 min")
  • Format: live session, video course, phishing simulation, or written policy acknowledgment
  • Attendee name and role
  • Completion confirmation: a signature, quiz score, or platform export
  • Trainer or provider
  • Next due date

A minimum program for a small business

  1. At hire: security basics plus signing the acceptable use policy.
  2. Every year: a 30 to 45 minute refresher on phishing, passwords and MFA, payment fraud, and how to report problems.
  3. Quarterly (optional): a simulated phishing email, with short follow-up training for anyone who clicks.
  4. After an incident: a short lesson on what happened, without naming or blaming anyone.

A 30-minute session you can run yourself

  1. 5 min: why it matters, using a real (anonymized) phishing email your business received.
  2. 10 min: walk through the 10 red flags and ask people to spot them in two more examples.
  3. 5 min: the two rules that matter most: deny MFA prompts you didn't start, and call to verify any payment change.
  4. 5 min: how to report, and that honest mistakes reported quickly won't be punished.
  5. 5 min: questions, then everyone signs the log.

Prefer short monthly sessions?

Instead of one long annual session, many small businesses do 10 to 15 minutes a month at a team meeting. Log each one the same way. A ready-made rotation of twelve topics, including newer scams like QR codes, fake IT support and deepfake voices, is in the 12-month training plan.

Setting the log up in a spreadsheet

  • One row per person per session, not one row per session. Questionnaires usually ask whether all staff were trained, and per-person rows answer that instantly.
  • Use drop-down lists for topic and format so entries stay consistent and easy to filter.
  • Add a "days overdue" column that compares the next due date with today, and filter or color it so overdue people stand out.
  • Keep evidence alongside it: a folder with sign-in sheets, quiz exports or simulation reports, named by date.
  • Restrict editing to the person who maintains it, so the record stays trustworthy.

Track the number who reported the message as well as who clicked. A rising report count is the best sign training is working.

Policy acknowledgments count too

When staff sign a policy, such as the acceptable use policy, BYOD policy or password and MFA policy, log it with the date and policy version. When a policy changes, re-collect acknowledgments and log them again.

What reviewers usually ask

  • Do all employees receive security awareness training, and how often?
  • Do new hires receive training before or soon after getting system access? (See the onboarding checklist.)
  • Do you run phishing simulations?
  • Can you provide records?

The exact wording varies by insurer and client; see the cyber insurance questionnaire guide for how to answer accurately.

How long to keep the log

Keep logs for at least as long as your insurer or regulator might ask about them. Six years is a common conservative choice for healthcare, since HIPAA documentation is generally retained for six years. For other businesses, keeping at least the last three years covers most insurance renewals and client questionnaires.

Frequently asked questions

How often should employees get security awareness training?

At hire and at least once a year, with a short refresher after any incident. Many insurers ask whether training is annual.

Does security awareness training need to be documented?

Yes. Insurers, auditors, and regulators generally expect a dated record of who completed training and when.

Can a small business run its own security training?

Yes. A 30-minute live session covering phishing, MFA, payment fraud, and reporting counts if it is documented.

What topics should small business security training cover?

Phishing and fake sign-in pages, MFA prompts, payment and invoice fraud, passwords and password managers, lost devices, sending data to the wrong person, and how to report problems.

How should I set up a training log spreadsheet?

Use one row per person per session, drop-down lists for topic and format, a next-due date with an overdue flag, and a folder of supporting evidence such as sign-in sheets or quiz exports.

Should I log phishing simulation results?

Yes. Record the date, the scenario, how many received it, how many clicked, how many reported it and the follow-up. Rising report counts show training is working.

Incident response plan template for small business

By William Todd, IT support and cybersecurity engineer · Updated 2026-11-21

An incident response plan is a short document, often three to five pages for a small business, that tells your team exactly what to do when something goes wrong: who's in charge, who to call, how to contain the damage, and how to recover. Writing it before you need it is the difference between a bad day and a business-ending week, and it's one of the documents cyber insurers ask about most.

Below is a free outline you can turn into your own plan: severity levels, the six response steps, a contact sheet, short playbooks for the most common incidents and an incident log. Print a copy, because during ransomware your computers may not work.

1. Roles

  • Incident lead (usually the owner or office manager) and a named backup. One person makes decisions and speaks for the business.
  • Technical lead: your IT provider or MSP, with their emergency number.
  • Outside help: your cyber insurer's breach hotline, which often assigns an incident response firm and a breach lawyer.

3. The six response steps

  1. Identify: confirm what happened, when, and which systems and data are affected. Start the incident log now.
  2. Contain: disconnect affected devices from the network without powering them off, disable compromised accounts, and stop any pending payments.
  3. Preserve: keep logs, emails (with headers), screenshots and photos of ransom notes. Don't wipe or reinstall anything yet.
  4. Eradicate: remove malware, reset credentials, revoke sessions and fix the weakness that let them in.
  5. Recover: restore from known-good backups, starting with the systems the business needs most, and watch for recurrence.
  6. Review: within a week, hold a lessons-learned meeting and update controls and this plan.

5. Short playbooks for common incidents

  • Clicked a phishing link or entered a password: first 15 minutes, then the mailbox cleanup checklist.
  • Ransomware: isolate, call the insurer, don't pay or wipe. See the ransomware first hour.
  • Fake invoice or bank-detail change paid: call the bank to recall the payment immediately, then follow the payment fraud steps.
  • Lost or stolen device: confirm whether it was encrypted, remotely lock or wipe it, reset the user's passwords, and record what data was on it.
  • Data sent to the wrong person: ask the recipient to delete it and confirm in writing, record what was sent, and check notification duties with counsel.

Notifications

Notification rules after a data breach vary by state, industry and the type of data, and many have deadlines measured in days. HIPAA, the FTC Safeguards Rule, PCI DSS and state breach laws can all apply. Contact your insurer and a breach lawyer early, before you notify customers.

Keep it alive

  • Review the plan and contact sheet every year and after any incident.
  • Run a one-hour tabletop exercise yearly so people know the plan exists.
  • Keep a printed copy somewhere you can reach without a computer.

Frequently asked questions

What should a small business incident response plan include?

Contacts, severity levels, the steps to identify, contain, preserve evidence, eradicate, recover, and review, plus an incident log.

Should I turn off a computer infected with ransomware?

Generally disconnect it from the network but do not power it off until evidence is preserved, unless your IT provider advises otherwise.

How long should an incident response plan be?

For a small business, two to four pages is enough if it lists contacts and clear steps.

How often should an incident response plan be tested?

At least once a year with a short tabletop exercise, and reviewed after any real incident or major change in systems or staff.

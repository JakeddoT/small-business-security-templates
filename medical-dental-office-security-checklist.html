Medical and dental office security checklist: 9 basics for small practices

By William Todd, IT support and cybersecurity engineer · Updated 2026-11-21

Small medical and dental practices hold some of the most sensitive data there is, usually without an IT department. Start with these nine basics: know where patient data lives, unique logins, MFA, encryption, vendor agreements, tested backups, staff training, a breach plan and a yearly risk assessment. Together they address the causes behind most healthcare breaches at small practices: phishing, ransomware, lost devices and vendors.

Use the checklist as a starting point for your HIPAA work, not a replacement for it. It's general information, not legal advice or a guarantee of HIPAA compliance.

The 9 basics

  1. Know where patient data lives. EHR, email, file shares, laptops, phones, imaging PCs, scanners, fax, printers, backups and paper. You can't protect what you haven't listed. This inventory is step one of your risk assessment.
  2. Unique logins for everyone. No shared front-desk or "doctor" account in the EHR or on PCs. Shared logins make it impossible to see who viewed a chart, and HIPAA expects unique user identification.
  3. MFA on email and remote access. Email takeover is one of the most common ways practices get breached. Turn on MFA for every mailbox and for any remote access to the office or EHR. See which accounts need MFA.
  4. Encrypt laptops and phones. Under HIPAA's breach rules, losing a properly encrypted device generally isn't a reportable breach; losing an unencrypted one may be. Check every PC with the BitLocker check.
  5. Track vendors and BAAs. List every vendor that touches patient data (EHR, billing, IT, answering service, labs, cloud backup) with the date of their business associate agreement.
  6. Tested backups with one copy offline or immutable. Ransomware targets backups first. Test a real restore at least quarterly and keep the log. See the backup restore test checklist.
  7. Train staff at hire and yearly, and document it. Phishing, fax and email mistakes, verifying callers, and reporting incidents. Keep a training log.
  8. Have a breach checklist ready. HIPAA requires notifying affected patients without unreasonable delay and no later than 60 days after discovering a breach, with additional reports to HHS (and the media for larger breaches). State laws may be stricter. Contact counsel promptly.
  9. Do a risk assessment yearly and after major changes like a new EHR, a move or a security incident.

Imaging and equipment PCs

X-ray sensors, intraoral cameras and lab equipment often run on PCs with old Windows versions that the equipment vendor won't let you update. Don't ignore them:

  • Put them on a separate network segment if your IT provider can.
  • Don't use them for email or web browsing.
  • Ask the vendor in writing what security updates are supported, and note the answer in your risk assessment.

If something goes wrong

  • Clicked a phishing link: follow the first 15 minutes.
  • Ransom note on screen: follow the ransomware first hour.
  • Then call your IT provider, your cyber insurer and your HIPAA counsel, in that order, and start your incident log.

Frequently asked questions

Do small medical offices need a security risk assessment?

HIPAA requires covered entities to conduct a risk analysis. A qualified compliance professional can confirm what applies to your practice.

Can front-desk staff share one login?

Shared logins make it impossible to know who accessed records. Each workforce member should have a unique login.

What security does a small dental office need?

At minimum: unique logins, MFA on email and remote access, encrypted laptops, business associate agreements with vendors, tested offline backups, yearly staff training, a breach plan and a yearly HIPAA risk assessment.

Is a lost encrypted laptop a HIPAA breach?

Generally, if the device was encrypted to the standard HHS guidance describes and the key wasn't compromised, the data is considered secured and the loss usually isn't a reportable breach. An unencrypted device may be. Confirm with counsel.

How long does a practice have to report a HIPAA breach?

Affected individuals must be notified without unreasonable delay and no later than 60 days after discovery. Breaches affecting 500 or more people also require notifying HHS and, in some cases, the media within that time. State laws may add requirements.

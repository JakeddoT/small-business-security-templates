Acceptable use policy template for small business: what to include

By William Todd, IT support and cybersecurity engineer · Updated 2026-11-21

An acceptable use policy (AUP) is a one-to-two-page document that tells staff what they can and can't do with company computers, accounts, email, internet and data, and what happens if they don't follow it. Every employee signs it at hire and again each year. It's usually the first policy an insurer, client or auditor asks to see.

Without one, you have nothing to point to when someone installs a cracked app that brings in ransomware, forwards customer files to their personal Gmail, or shares a password with a friend. The AUP makes the rules explicit and gives you a fair basis for enforcing them.

The 8 sections to include

  1. Purpose and scope. Who it covers (employees, contractors, temps, interns) and what (company devices, accounts, networks, data, and personal devices used for work).
  2. Acceptable use. Systems are for business purposes, with limited personal use allowed if it doesn't interfere with work or security. Credentials stay private. Screens lock when you walk away.
  3. Prohibited use. Installing unapproved software or browser extensions; disabling antivirus, updates or other security tools; sharing passwords or MFA codes; copying company data to personal email, USB drives or personal cloud storage; accessing data you don't need for your job; illegal, harassing or offensive content.
  4. Email and phishing. Don't open unexpected attachments or approve MFA prompts you didn't start. Verify payment or bank-detail changes by phone (see vendor bank change verification). Report suspicious emails.
  5. Data handling. Store files only in approved locations. Don't email sensitive data unencrypted. Follow the data classification and retention policy.
  6. Incident reporting. Report lost devices, suspicious emails, clicked links and anything odd immediately, without fear of punishment for honest mistakes. Fast reporting is what limits damage.
  7. Monitoring and privacy. Company systems may be monitored for security and compliance, to the extent the law allows. Staff shouldn't expect privacy on company accounts.
  8. Enforcement and acknowledgment. Violations may lead to loss of access or discipline up to termination. A signature and date line for each employee.

Sample wording you can adapt

Employees must not install software, browser extensions or apps on company devices unless they are on the approved list or approved in writing by [IT contact]. Employees must not copy, forward or upload company or customer data to personal email accounts, personal cloud storage or removable media.
Employees must report any suspected security incident, including a lost or stolen device, a suspicious email, or a link or attachment they opened by mistake, to [contact] immediately and in any case within 24 hours. Reporting an honest mistake promptly will not result in discipline.

AUP vs other policies

PolicyCoversWho reads it
Acceptable use policyDay-to-day rules for all staffEveryone, at hire and yearly
Password and MFA policyPassword length, password manager, MFA scopeEveryone; IT enforces it
BYOD and remote work policyPersonal phones, laptops and home networksAnyone using personal devices or working remotely
Incident response planWhat happens after an incidentOwner, managers, IT provider

Small businesses often fold the essentials of the password and BYOD rules into the AUP so staff only sign one document. That's fine, as long as the detailed versions exist for IT and your insurer.

Rolling it out

  • Keep it short. Two pages that people read beat ten pages they sign blind.
  • Get signatures and keep them. Store signed copies with HR files, and record yearly re-acknowledgment in your training log. Insurers and auditors look for the evidence, not just the policy.
  • Make it fit reality. If you ban personal use entirely and everyone ignores it, the policy is weaker for it. Allow reasonable personal use and ban the risky things specifically.
  • Review yearly and whenever you add a major system, such as a new cloud app or remote access tool.

Employment and monitoring rules vary by state and country, so have an employment lawyer review the monitoring and enforcement wording before you rely on it.

Frequently asked questions

Do employees need to sign an acceptable use policy?

It is best practice to collect a signed acknowledgment at hire and annually, as evidence that employees were informed.

What is prohibited in a typical acceptable use policy?

Installing unapproved software, disabling security tools, sharing credentials, copying company data to personal storage, and accessing systems without authorization.

What should an acceptable use policy include?

Purpose and scope, acceptable and prohibited use, email and phishing rules, data handling, incident reporting, monitoring and privacy, and enforcement with a signature line for each employee.

Does a small business need an acceptable use policy?

It's strongly recommended. Cyber insurers, clients and auditors commonly ask for one, and it gives you a clear basis for enforcing security rules with staff.

How often should employees sign the acceptable use policy?

At hire and once a year, and again after any significant change to the policy. Keep the signed copies as evidence.

PCI compliance checklist for small business: what card merchants need to do

By William Todd, IT support and cybersecurity engineer · Updated 2026-11-21

If you accept credit or debit cards, your payment processor requires you to follow PCI DSS (the Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard) and usually to complete a yearly Self-Assessment Questionnaire (SAQ). For most small merchants, compliance means knowing which SAQ applies, keeping card data out of your own systems, securing your terminals and network, and filing the SAQ and attestation each year.

PCI DSS is a card-industry standard, not a law, but processors enforce it through your merchant agreement, often with monthly "non-compliance" fees, and a breach without it can be very expensive. The current version is PCI DSS v4.0.1. Confirm your SAQ type with your processor; the descriptions below are a simplified guide.

Step 1: Find your SAQ type

How you take cardsLikely SAQ
Online only, and the payment page is fully hosted by your processor (redirect or iframe)SAQ A
Online, but your website controls or affects the payment page (for example a direct-post form)SAQ A-EP
Standalone terminals that connect by phone line, or manual imprint machinesSAQ B
Standalone, PCI-approved terminals connected over the internet, not to your POS PCSAQ B-IP
Keying cards one at a time into a processor's web-based virtual terminalSAQ C-VT
A validated point-to-point encryption (P2PE) solution from your processorSAQ P2PE (usually the shortest)
A POS system on a PC or network connected to the internetSAQ C (or D)
Anything else, including storing card dataSAQ D (the longest)

The biggest win is reducing scope. Moving to a validated P2PE terminal or a hosted payment page can turn a 200-question SAQ into a short one, and it reduces real risk too.

Step 2: The checklist

Card data

  • Never store full card numbers, CVV codes or magnetic stripe data after authorization. Let the processor tokenize and store cards.
  • Never write card numbers on paper, or accept them by email, text or chat. If a customer emails one, delete it and reply asking them to pay another way.
  • Shred any paper with card data immediately.

Network

  • Keep payment terminals and POS systems on a network separate from guest Wi-Fi and office PCs.
  • Use a firewall that blocks inbound connections by default.
  • Change default passwords on routers, Wi-Fi, terminals and POS software.
  • Remote access to POS systems uses MFA and named accounts, never a shared vendor password. See why exposed remote desktop is dangerous.

Terminals and devices

  • Keep an inventory of every terminal and card reader, with make, model, serial number and location.
  • Inspect them for tampering and skimmers on a schedule, and log it. See the 2-minute skimmer check.
  • Patch POS PCs and keep antivirus on. Don't use them for email or web browsing.

People and access

  • Every employee has a unique POS login. No shared manager codes.
  • Remove access on the employee's last day.
  • Train staff yearly on skimmers, phishing and social engineering ("I'm from the processor, I need to update your terminal"), and keep a training log.

Every year

  • Complete the correct SAQ and the Attestation of Compliance (AOC), usually through your processor's portal.
  • If your SAQ requires it, run quarterly external vulnerability scans with an Approved Scanning Vendor (ASV). Your processor often provides one.
  • Get your POS vendor's and processor's PCI attestations, and keep them on file.
  • Review your written security policies and your incident response plan.

If you suspect a card breach

  1. Don't turn off or wipe the POS systems. Disconnect them from the network.
  2. Call your processor or acquiring bank right away. They'll tell you the required next steps, which may include a forensic investigation.
  3. Call your cyber insurer and follow your incident response plan.

Common mistakes

  • Ticking the wrong SAQ. The SAQ must match how you actually take cards, including phone orders and any online store.
  • Forgetting the phone. Writing phone-order card numbers on a notepad puts paper in scope.
  • Vendor remote access left open. Many POS breaches start with a vendor's always-on remote tool and a shared password.

Frequently asked questions

Do small businesses need to be PCI compliant?

Yes. Any business that accepts card payments is expected to comply with PCI DSS, usually by completing a Self-Assessment Questionnaire.

How can a small store reduce PCI scope?

Use a validated point-to-point encryption or tokenizing payment solution and segment the payment network.

Which PCI SAQ do I need as a small business?

It depends on how you accept cards. Fully outsourced online payments usually mean SAQ A, standalone internet-connected terminals SAQ B-IP, validated P2PE terminals SAQ P2PE, and POS systems on internet-connected PCs SAQ C or D. Confirm with your processor.

Is PCI compliance required by law?

PCI DSS is an industry standard, not a law, but card brands and processors require it through your merchant agreement, and some states reference it. Non-compliance can bring fees and liability after a breach.

How can a small business reduce PCI scope?

Use a validated point-to-point encryption (P2PE) terminal or a processor-hosted payment page, never store card data, and keep payment systems on a separate network.

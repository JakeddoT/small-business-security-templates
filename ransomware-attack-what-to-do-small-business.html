Ransomware attack? What a small business should do in the first hour

By William Todd, IT support and cybersecurity engineer · Updated 2026-11-21

Disconnect the affected computers from the network now (unplug the cable, turn off Wi-Fi), but leave them powered on. Then call your cyber insurer's breach hotline or your IT provider before you do anything else. Don't pay, don't wipe, and don't start restoring yet. Those decisions come later, and making them in the first ten minutes is how small businesses lose evidence, coverage or their backups.

Print this page and keep it with your incident response plan. During an attack, your computers may not be usable.

First 15 minutes: contain

  1. Isolate, don't shut down. Pull the network cable and turn off Wi-Fi on every computer showing the ransom note or renamed files. Powering off can destroy evidence investigators use. CISA's guidance is to power devices down only if you can't disconnect them from the network.
  2. Isolate the backups. Disconnect any USB or network backup drive. Ransomware goes after backups first.
  3. Stop the spread. If several machines are affected or you can't tell, unplug the internet connection at the router or firewall.
  4. Take photos. Photograph the ransom note and any messages with your phone. Write down the time and what you saw.
  5. Name one person in charge. One person makes decisions and talks to outsiders. Everyone else stops trying fixes.

Next 45 minutes: call the right people, in order

  1. Your cyber insurer's claims or breach hotline (on your policy or your broker's site). Many policies require prompt notice and expect you to use their approved incident response firm and lawyers. Hiring your own first can cost you coverage.
  2. Your IT provider or MSP. Tell them it's ransomware and that the insurer has been called, so they coordinate instead of wiping.
  3. Law enforcement. Report to the FBI at ic3.gov or your local FBI field office, and to CISA at cisa.gov/report. They sometimes have decryption keys or intelligence on the group.
  4. Your bank, if accounting or banking computers are involved. Ask them to watch for unusual transfers.

Use a phone, not the affected computers or email, to make these calls. Assume the attacker can read your email.

What not to do

  • Don't pay or contact the attackers yourself. Payment decisions involve your insurer, lawyers and sometimes sanctions checks (the U.S. Treasury's OFAC has warned that paying some groups can violate sanctions). Paying also doesn't guarantee your data back.
  • Don't wipe or reinstall before the incident responder says so. You'll destroy the evidence of how they got in, and they'll get in again the same way.
  • Don't plug backups back in to "check them" while infected machines are still on the network.
  • Don't run random decryptors from the internet. Legitimate free decryptors for some strains exist through the No More Ransom project, but let your responder identify the strain first.
  • Don't announce it on social media or email all customers yet. Notification is a legal question with deadlines that depend on your state and industry. Your insurer's breach lawyer handles it.

After it's over: the 5 fixes that prevent most repeats

  1. MFA on email, remote access and admin accounts. See which accounts insurers expect MFA on.
  2. No remote desktop (RDP) open to the internet.
  3. At least one offline or immutable backup, tested quarterly.
  4. Patching within a few weeks, faster for anything internet-facing.
  5. A one-hour tabletop exercise once a year, so everyone knows this page exists.

Frequently asked questions

Should I turn off my computer if I have ransomware?

Disconnect it from the network first and leave it powered on, since shutting down can destroy evidence. CISA's guidance is to power devices off only if you can't disconnect them from the network.

Should a small business pay the ransom?

Don't decide alone. Involve your cyber insurer and a breach lawyer, since payment can carry sanctions risk and doesn't guarantee recovery. Most businesses with tested backups recover without paying.

Who do I report a ransomware attack to?

Your cyber insurer first (if you have one), then the FBI through ic3.gov or a local field office, and CISA at cisa.gov/report. Your breach lawyer will advise on customer and regulator notifications.

Can I restore from backup right away?

Not until the entry point is found and closed and infected machines are isolated. Otherwise the attacker can re-encrypt your restored systems, including the backups.

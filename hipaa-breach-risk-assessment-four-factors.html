HIPAA breach risk assessment: the four factors, with examples

By William Todd, IT support and cybersecurity engineer · Updated 2026-11-21

Under HIPAA, an impermissible use or disclosure of unsecured protected health information (PHI) is presumed to be a breach unless you can show a low probability that the PHI was compromised, based on a risk assessment of at least four factors: (1) the nature and extent of the PHI involved, (2) the unauthorized person who used it or received it, (3) whether the PHI was actually acquired or viewed, and (4) the extent to which the risk has been mitigated. The factors come from the Breach Notification Rule's definition of breach (45 CFR 164.402). You have to write the assessment down, and the burden of showing low probability is on you.

This page is for small medical, dental, therapy and other practices that need to decide quickly whether an incident is reportable. It's general information, not legal advice. For anything borderline, involving many patients, or involving a criminal, talk to a healthcare lawyer or your cyber insurer's breach coach.

First question: was the PHI "unsecured"?

Breach notification applies to unsecured PHI. PHI that was encrypted in line with HHS guidance (with the key not compromised), or properly destroyed, is not unsecured. That's why an encrypted laptop that goes missing is usually not a reportable breach, while an unencrypted one often is (see checking BitLocker and lost laptop steps).

The four factors in plain English

FactorQuestions to answerLower riskHigher risk
1. Nature and extent of the PHIWhat identifiers and clinical details? How sensitive? Could it be re-identified?Appointment time and first name onlySSNs, insurance IDs, diagnoses, mental health or substance use details
2. Who received itAnother HIPAA-covered organization? An employee? A stranger? A criminal?Another covered provider bound by HIPAAUnknown person, ex-employee, or attacker
3. Actually acquired or viewed?Is there evidence it was opened, downloaded or read?Envelope returned unopened; logs show no accessFiles downloaded; email opened and forwarded
4. MitigationDid you get it back or get written assurance it was deleted and not used?Recipient signed a statement confirming deletionNo response, or data posted or sold

Assess each factor separately, then reach an overall conclusion. No single factor decides it on its own.

If it is a breach: notification deadlines

WhoWhen
Affected individualsWithout unreasonable delay and no later than 60 calendar days after discovery
HHS, fewer than 500 peopleLog it, and report to HHS within 60 days after the end of the calendar year
HHS, 500 or more peopleAt the same time as the individual notices (within 60 days of discovery)
MediaWhen more than 500 residents of a state or jurisdiction are affected
Your business associates' breachesThey must notify you; your BAA should set a shorter deadline

State breach laws may add their own requirements and deadlines. "Discovery" means the day the breach is known, or would have been known with reasonable diligence, to anyone in your practice other than the person who caused it.

Prevent the next one

  • Encrypt every laptop and portable drive, and keep proof that it's on.
  • Use a secure portal or encrypted email for PHI, and turn on recipient warnings (see sent email to the wrong person).
  • MFA on email and the practice management system.
  • Train staff and log it, and review the security risk assessment annually.

Frequently asked questions

What are the four factors in a HIPAA breach risk assessment?

The nature and extent of the PHI involved, the unauthorized person who used or received it, whether the PHI was actually acquired or viewed, and the extent to which the risk has been mitigated.

Is a lost encrypted laptop a HIPAA breach?

Generally not, if the encryption met HHS guidance and the key or password wasn't compromised, because the PHI isn't considered unsecured. Document the evidence that encryption was on.

How long do I have to report a HIPAA breach?

Notify affected individuals without unreasonable delay and no later than 60 days after discovery. Breaches of 500 or more people go to HHS at the same time; smaller breaches are reported to HHS within 60 days after the end of the calendar year.

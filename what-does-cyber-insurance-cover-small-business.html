What does cyber insurance cover for a small business?

By William Todd, IT support and cybersecurity engineer · Updated 2026-11-22

A small-business cyber insurance policy typically has two halves. First-party cover pays your own costs after an incident: forensic investigation, breach lawyers, notifying customers, restoring data and systems, lost income while you're down, and sometimes ransomware negotiation and payment. Third-party cover pays for claims against you: lawsuits from customers or partners, some regulatory fines and penalties where insurable, and card-industry assessments. Social engineering and funds transfer fraud are often limited, optional or sub-limited, so read that part carefully. Every policy is different; this page explains the usual parts so you can ask your broker the right questions.

This is general information, not insurance advice. Your policy wording and your broker decide what's covered.

Common exclusions and conditions

  • Inaccurate application answers. If you said you had MFA everywhere and didn't, a claim can be challenged (see the questionnaire guide and MFA requirements).
  • Unpatched or unsupported systems in some policies, especially where a known vulnerability was ignored.
  • War and state-backed attack exclusions, worded differently across insurers.
  • Prior known incidents that started before the policy.
  • Betterment: upgrading systems beyond what you had before usually isn't covered.
  • Bodily injury and property damage, usually excluded.

Questions to ask your broker

  1. What are the overall limit and the retention (deductible)?
  2. Which covers have sub-limits, especially ransomware, social engineering and PCI?
  3. What conditions apply to funds transfer fraud cover?
  4. What's the business interruption waiting period, and does it cover dependent vendors?
  5. Must I use panel vendors, and do I need consent before spending?
  6. Is there a war or state-actor exclusion, and how is it worded?
  7. Does the policy cover regulatory defense, and fines where insurable?
  8. What security controls are conditions of cover?

How to keep cover affordable

  • Put the controls insurers ask about in place before renewal: MFA, EDR, backups you can restore, no exposed RDP (see the ransomware prevention checklist and EDR vs antivirus).
  • Start renewal prep early (see the renewal checklist).
  • Document a call-back procedure for payment changes (see bank detail change verification).
  • Know what to do if something happens (see filing a cyber insurance claim).

Frequently asked questions

What does small business cyber insurance usually cover?

First-party costs such as incident response, forensics, data restoration, notification, business interruption and sometimes ransomware, plus third-party liability for claims against you. Social engineering fraud is often limited or optional.

Does cyber insurance cover wire fraud and fake invoices?

Sometimes, under social engineering or funds transfer fraud cover, which is often sub-limited and may require a documented call-back procedure. Check your policy wording.

What can make a cyber insurance claim be denied?

Common issues include inaccurate application answers about controls such as MFA, late notice, using non-panel vendors without consent, exclusions such as war or prior incidents, and policy conditions that weren't met.

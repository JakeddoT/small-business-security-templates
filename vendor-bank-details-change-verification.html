Vendor changed bank details? How to verify it before you pay

By William Todd, IT support and cybersecurity engineer · Updated 2026-11-21

Never accept a change to a vendor's bank details from an email alone. Call the vendor on a phone number you already had on file, not one from the email or invoice, and confirm the change with someone you know before paying a single invoice to the new account.

Fake "we've changed banks" requests are one of the most common forms of business email compromise (BEC). The email often comes from the vendor's real mailbox, because the vendor was hacked first. It may reply in a real thread about a real invoice. Spam filters, sender checks and "it looked legit" all fail here. A phone call to a known number doesn't.

The 5-step verification procedure

  1. Freeze the change. Don't update the vendor record or pay to the new account yet. Pay any urgent invoice to the old account, or hold it.
  2. Call a known number. Use the number in your vendor file, on an old invoice you already paid, or on the vendor's website typed in yourself. Never use a number, link or contact from the change request.
  3. Talk to someone you know. Ask your usual contact, by name, to confirm the change and read you the last 4 digits of the new account. You read nothing to them.
  4. Get a second approval. A second person (owner, office manager or bookkeeper) signs off before the record changes. One person should never be able to change bank details and release a payment.
  5. Log it and watch the first payment. Record who confirmed it, how and when. After the first payment to the new account, confirm with the vendor that it arrived.

Red flags in a bank change request

  • Urgency: "pay today or the shipment is held", "our old account is frozen".
  • A new account in a different country, a different company name or a personal name.
  • A slightly different email domain (acme-supply.co vs acmesupply.com) or a Reply-To that doesn't match the sender.
  • Requests to keep it quiet, skip the usual process or reply only by email.
  • A changed invoice PDF with new bank details but the same invoice number.
  • A request that arrives right after a real invoice or payment conversation. Attackers read the thread and time it.

Sample verification log

Keep one line per change. It takes 30 seconds and gives your insurer, bank and accountant evidence that you followed a procedure.

The second row is the one that matters: the vendor said they never sent it. That's a confirmed fraud attempt, and the vendor needs to know their mailbox is probably compromised.

Already paid the new account? The first hour

  1. Call your bank's fraud line now and ask them to recall the payment. Speed matters more than anything else. FinCEN has said recovery is most likely when fraudulent wires are reported to law enforcement within 72 hours, and earlier is better.
  2. File a complaint at ic3.gov (the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center) with the amount, date, receiving bank and account details, and the emails.
  3. Tell the real vendor on a known number. Their email account is likely compromised, and other customers may be targeted.
  4. Check your own email for forwarding rules or sign-ins you don't recognize, and reset the password with MFA on. The attacker may be in your mailbox instead of the vendor's.
  5. Call your cyber insurer or broker. Many policies need prompt notice, and some cover funds transfer fraud only if you followed a verification procedure. That's another reason to keep the log.
  6. Save everything. Keep the emails (with headers), the invoice PDFs and the payment confirmation. Don't delete the thread.

Policy wording you can adopt

Put this in your finance procedures or acceptable use policy:

Changes to a vendor's or employee's payment details are made only after verbal confirmation with a known contact on a phone number already on file. The person who confirms the change records it in the verification log, and a second authorized person approves it before any payment is made to the new details. Requests received by email, text or chat alone are never sufficient.

The same rule covers payroll: "please send my direct deposit to this new account" emails are the same scam aimed at HR.

Common mistakes

  • Replying to the email to confirm. You're asking the attacker.
  • Calling the number in the email signature. Attackers change signatures too.
  • Treating it as an IT problem only. It's a payment control. Your bookkeeper owns it as much as IT does.
  • Skipping it for small or trusted vendors. Attackers pick long-standing vendors precisely because nobody questions them.

Frequently asked questions

How do I verify a vendor's bank account change?

Call the vendor on a phone number you already had on file, not one from the request, and confirm the change with a contact you know. Have a second person approve it, log it, and confirm the first payment arrived.

What should I do if I paid a fraudulent invoice?

Call your bank's fraud line immediately to request a recall, file a complaint at ic3.gov, tell the real vendor on a known number, check your own mailbox for forwarding rules, and notify your cyber insurer.

Can a vendor change request be fake if it comes from their real email address?

Yes. In many cases the vendor's mailbox was hacked first, so the request comes from their real address and even replies in a real thread. That's why verification has to happen by phone to a known number.

Does cyber insurance cover funds transfer fraud?

Some policies include social engineering or funds transfer fraud coverage, often with a lower sub-limit, and some require that you followed a verification procedure. Check your policy wording with your broker.

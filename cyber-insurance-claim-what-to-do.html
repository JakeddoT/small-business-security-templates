Cyber insurance claim: what to do after an incident

By William Todd, IT support and cybersecurity engineer · Updated 2026-11-22

If you have cyber insurance and suspect an incident, call the insurer's claims or breach hotline as early as possible, before you hire outside IT forensics, a lawyer or a negotiator and before you pay any ransom or extortion demand. Most policies require prompt notice, and many expect you to use the insurer's approved (panel) vendors or get consent before spending money. Meanwhile, contain the problem without destroying evidence, and keep a written timeline. Read your own policy for the exact rules; they vary a lot.

This page is general information, not insurance or legal advice. Your policy wording, your broker and the insurer's claims team decide what's covered.

Before anything happens: find these now

  • The insurer's claims or breach hotline number and your policy number, written down somewhere you can reach if email and computers are down.
  • Your broker's contact details.
  • Whether the policy has a panel of approved vendors (breach lawyer, forensics, ransomware negotiation, PR) and whether you must use them.
  • Your deductible or retention, and any waiting period for business interruption cover.
  • Whether social engineering or funds transfer fraud is covered, and what verification conditions apply (see bank detail change verification).

Put these in your incident response plan so whoever is on hand at 2 a.m. knows who to call.

When an incident happens

  1. Contain, don't destroy. Disconnect affected computers from the network, but don't wipe, reinstall or turn everything off unless your IT provider or the insurer's forensics team tells you to (see the ransomware first hour).
  2. Call the claims or breach hotline. Explain what you know, and ask what they need from you and which vendors to use.
  3. Don't pay a ransom or extortion demand, or hire a negotiator, without the insurer's involvement. Payments can have legal and sanctions implications, and paying without consent may affect cover.
  4. Don't admit liability to customers or third parties before talking to the insurer and breach lawyer. You can still warn people to protect themselves.
  5. For payment fraud, call your bank immediately to try to recall the payment, and file a report at ic3.gov, then notify the insurer.
  6. Keep a timeline: when you discovered it, who you told, what you did, and costs incurred.

Common pitfalls

  • Late notice. Waiting until you're sure it's serious can cause problems. Report suspected incidents and let the insurer help decide.
  • Hiring your own vendors first when the policy requires panel vendors or consent, which can leave costs unreimbursed.
  • Application answers that weren't true. If you said you had MFA on all email and didn't, expect questions. Keep your answers accurate at every renewal (see the questionnaire guide and MFA requirements).
  • Restoring over the evidence before forensics has what it needs.
  • Forgetting notification duties. Breaches of personal data can trigger legal notification deadlines; the breach lawyer will advise (for healthcare, see the HIPAA breach risk assessment).

After the claim

  • Fix what let it happen, and write down what you changed.
  • Run a short review with staff and update the plan; a tabletop exercise is a good way to test the updates.
  • Expect the incident to come up at renewal, and prepare evidence of improvements (see the renewal checklist).

Frequently asked questions

When should I contact my cyber insurer after an incident?

As early as possible, as soon as you suspect an incident, and before hiring outside vendors or paying any ransom. Most policies require prompt notice; check your policy for the exact terms.

Can I use my own IT company after a cyber incident if I have insurance?

Your IT provider can usually help contain the problem, but many policies require you to use the insurer's panel vendors or get consent for forensics, legal and negotiation costs. Ask the claims team first.

What evidence do I need for a cyber insurance claim?

Ransom notes or scam messages, system and sign-in logs, a dated timeline of what happened and what you did, invoices for incident costs, and records of lost revenue.

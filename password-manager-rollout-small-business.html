Password manager rollout for small business: a 30-day plan

By William Todd, IT support and cybersecurity engineer · Updated 2026-11-21

To roll out a password manager in a small business: pick a business plan (not personal accounts) with admin controls and shared vaults, set it up yourself first, move the shared company logins into it, then bring staff on in small groups with a 20-minute walkthrough. Require MFA on every password manager account, and build it into onboarding and offboarding. Most businesses can do this in about 30 days without disrupting work.

A password manager fixes the problems that cause a lot of small-business incidents: reused passwords, the shared spreadsheet of logins, the sticky note on the monitor, and the bank portal password that only the person who left knew. It also makes long, unique passwords practical, which is what current NIST guidance favors over forced regular changes.

Business features to require

Well-known options with business plans include 1Password, Bitwarden, Keeper, Dashlane and others. Compare current features and prices on their own sites, and avoid letting staff use personal password manager accounts for company logins, since the business can't recover or remove those.

The 30-day plan

  1. Days 1–3: set up. Create the business account, turn on MFA enforcement, set up account recovery for at least two admins, and decide your vault structure (for example: Everyone, Finance, Admin, Marketing).
  2. Days 4–7: move the shared logins. Start with the accounts that would hurt most to lose: bank and payroll, domain registrar and DNS, email admin, accounting, social media, key vendor portals. Change each password to a new generated one as you move it, and turn on MFA where the service supports it.
  3. Days 8–14: pilot group. Two or three people, including your least technical colleague. Fix the friction they find.
  4. Days 15–25: everyone else. A 20-minute session in small groups: install, save the first login, use autofill, share an item. Delete the old password spreadsheet once it's empty, and empty the trash.
  5. Days 26–30: check. Use the admin reports to see who hasn't joined and which passwords are still weak or reused. Add it to your onboarding and offboarding checklists, and record the rollout in your training log.

Rules to give staff

All company passwords live in the company password manager. Use a generated password for every work account. Never reuse a work password anywhere else. Never share passwords by email, text or chat; share them in the password manager. Your master password is yours alone; make it a long passphrase and never write it down at your desk.

Put the full rules in your password and MFA policy, which can point to the password manager as the approved way to store and share credentials.

The master password

  • Use a long passphrase: several random words is easier to remember and harder to guess than a short complex password.
  • It's the one password people have to remember, so it must not be used anywhere else.
  • MFA on top of it is not optional; an authenticator app or security key is better than SMS where available.
  • Make sure the business recovery option is set up before anyone gets locked out.

Offboarding and emergencies

  • Last day: remove or suspend the person in the password manager admin console, then rotate every shared password they could see. The vault's access list tells you exactly which ones (see the offboarding checklist).
  • Owner emergency: make sure a trusted second person can get into the critical logins if the owner is unavailable. Most business plans support a second admin or emergency access.
  • Lost phone or laptop: the vault is encrypted, but sign the device out remotely and check the account's activity (see lost laptop steps).

Common objections

  • "Isn't it risky to put all passwords in one place?" It's a trade-off, and for most businesses a good one. A reputable password manager with MFA is far safer than reused passwords, spreadsheets and browser-saved passwords on shared computers.
  • "Staff won't use it." They will if autofill works and the old spreadsheet is gone. The pilot group is there to find the friction.
  • "We already save passwords in the browser." Browser saving is better than reuse, but the business usually can't share, recover or remove those passwords centrally when someone leaves.

Frequently asked questions

Should a small business use a password manager?

Yes, for most businesses. A business password manager with MFA makes unique passwords practical, lets you share logins safely, and lets you remove access cleanly when someone leaves.

How do you roll out a password manager to employees?

Set it up and move the shared company logins first, pilot it with two or three people, then train everyone else in short small-group sessions, delete the old password spreadsheet, and add it to onboarding and offboarding.

Can employees use their personal password manager for work?

It's better not to. The business can't recover, audit or remove company logins stored in a personal account when someone leaves.

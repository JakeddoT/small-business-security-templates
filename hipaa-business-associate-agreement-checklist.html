HIPAA business associate agreement checklist for small practices

By William Todd, IT support and cybersecurity engineer · Updated 2026-11-21

Any vendor that creates, receives, maintains or transmits patient information on your behalf is a business associate under HIPAA, and you need a signed business associate agreement (BAA) with them before they get access. For a small medical or dental practice that usually means the EHR or practice management vendor, the IT provider, cloud backup and email, the billing company, the answering service and any shredding company.

Missing BAAs are a common finding in HHS enforcement, and they're easy to fix: list your vendors, find out who already has a BAA on file, and request the rest. This is general information, not legal advice.

Two common traps: HHS has said a cloud provider that stores ePHI is a business associate even if the data is encrypted and the provider doesn't have the key, and consumer versions of email or file-sharing services usually won't sign a BAA. Use the business plan of the service and sign its BAA.

What a BAA must include

HIPAA's rules for business associate contracts (45 CFR 164.504(e)) require the agreement to cover, among other things, that the business associate will:

  • use and disclose PHI only as the contract permits or the law requires;
  • use appropriate safeguards, including complying with the HIPAA Security Rule for ePHI;
  • report uses or disclosures not allowed by the contract, including breaches of unsecured PHI and security incidents;
  • make sure any subcontractors that handle the PHI agree to the same restrictions;
  • help you meet patients' rights to access, amend and get an accounting of disclosures, where applicable;
  • make its records available to HHS for compliance reviews;
  • return or destroy PHI at the end of the contract, where feasible;

and that you can terminate the contract if the business associate violates a material term. HHS publishes sample business associate agreement provisions you can compare against.

How to get it done this month

  1. List every vendor you pay (check your bank and card statements) and mark which ones touch patient data.
  2. Search your email and files for existing BAAs. Many cloud vendors provide theirs in the admin portal or on request.
  3. Email the rest: "We're a HIPAA covered entity. Please send your business associate agreement for signature."
  4. If a vendor won't sign one, stop sending them PHI and find an alternative.
  5. Record everything in the log and review it yearly as part of your risk assessment.

Beyond the paperwork

A BAA moves legal obligations; it doesn't make a vendor secure. For high-risk vendors (EHR, IT provider), also ask a few security questions: MFA on their access, breach notification timeframe, independent assessments. The vendor security questionnaire has 15 to choose from.

Frequently asked questions

Who needs a business associate agreement under HIPAA?

Any person or company that creates, receives, maintains or transmits protected health information on behalf of a covered entity, such as EHR vendors, IT providers, cloud storage and email providers, billing companies and answering services.

Do cloud providers need a BAA if the data is encrypted?

Yes, according to HHS guidance. A cloud provider that stores ePHI is a business associate even if the data is encrypted and the provider doesn't hold the key.

Does my internet provider need a BAA?

Usually not. Services that only transmit data, with only temporary storage incident to transmission, fall under the conduit exception. Services that store ePHI don't.

What happens if a vendor won't sign a BAA?

Don't share PHI with them. Use a vendor or service plan that will sign a BAA instead.

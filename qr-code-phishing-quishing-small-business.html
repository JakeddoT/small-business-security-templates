QR code phishing (quishing): what small businesses need to know

By William Todd, IT support and cybersecurity engineer · Updated 2026-11-21

QR code phishing, or "quishing", hides a malicious link inside a QR code so people scan it with their phone instead of clicking it on a protected work computer. For a small business the two common forms are emails asking staff to "scan to sign in" or "scan to view the document", and fake QR stickers placed over your own codes on tables, counters, signs or invoices. The fix is a simple staff rule (never scan a QR code to sign in to a work account), regular checks of the QR codes you display, and fast password resets if someone already scanned one.

The FTC has warned consumers about scammers covering legitimate QR codes, such as on parking meters, with their own, and about QR codes sent by text or email with an invented reason to scan. The same tricks work against a business's staff and customers.

Why QR codes get past defenses

  • The phone is usually less protected. A staff member scans a code from their work screen with a personal phone that may not have the same web filtering or security software as the work PC.
  • The link is an image. Email security tools are built to inspect text links; a link drawn as a picture can be harder to check, depending on the tool.
  • You can't see where it goes. A QR code looks the same whether it leads to your bank or a fake login page, so people judge it by the message around it.

Red flags in a QR code email

What the message saysWhat it's usually after
"Your MFA / password expires today, scan to keep access"Your Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace password and MFA code
"A document has been shared with you, scan to view"Email login on a fake sign-in page
"Payroll or benefits update, scan to confirm your details"Login, personal data or bank details (see the direct deposit scam)
"Invoice overdue, scan to pay"A payment to the scammer
A PDF attachment containing only a QR code and little textGetting the link past email filters

Real IT systems rarely ask you to sign in by scanning a QR code from an email. Treat any of these as phishing until it's confirmed another way (see the phishing red flags poster).

The staff rule

Never scan a QR code in an email, text or PDF to sign in to a work account, confirm details or make a payment. Go to the site or app the way you normally do. If you're not sure, ask before you scan, and report the message.

Add it to your acceptable use policy and your next training session, and record the training in your training log.

If someone already scanned one

  1. Ask what they did after scanning. Did they enter a password, an MFA code, card details, or install anything?
  2. If they entered a work password: change it straight away from a trusted device, sign the account out of all sessions, and check its MFA methods and mail rules (see the Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace checklist).
  3. If they paid or gave card details: call the bank or card issuer right away.
  4. If they installed an app: remove it, and have your IT provider check the phone, especially if it has work email on it.
  5. Warn colleagues, since the same message often goes to several people, and log the incident (see clicked a phishing link).

Protect the QR codes you display

If customers scan your codes for menus, payments, reviews, Wi-Fi or invoices, a sticker placed over yours can send them to a fake payment page under your name.

  • Check them regularly, for example as part of your opening checklist. Look for stickers, raised edges or codes that don't match.
  • Scan your own codes now and then and confirm they open your real address.
  • Print codes into the material (menus, signs, receipts) or put them behind a laminate or display stand rather than on loose stickers.
  • Show the web address next to the code so customers can type it instead, and confirm the address they land on.
  • On invoices, tell customers how you take payment and that you'll never change bank details by email (see bank detail change verification).

Technical settings that help

  • MFA on every email account, ideally phishing-resistant methods such as passkeys or security keys for admins (see the MFA rollout plan).
  • Ask your email security provider whether it scans QR codes in images and PDFs; many now advertise this.
  • If work email is on phones, use device management or app protection so the phone gets basic protection.

Frequently asked questions

What is quishing?

Quishing is phishing that hides a malicious link in a QR code, so the victim scans it with a phone instead of clicking a link. It's often used to steal email logins or payments.

How do I spot a QR code phishing email?

Be suspicious of any email, text or PDF asking you to scan a code to sign in, keep MFA working, view a document, confirm payroll details or pay an invoice. Go to the site the normal way instead.

What should I do if I scanned a phishing QR code?

If you entered a password, change it from a trusted device and sign out all sessions. If you paid or gave card details, call your bank. Then report it so colleagues are warned.
